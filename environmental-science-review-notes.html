AP Environmental Science Quick Review Notes

Based on the College Board course framework for 2026–27.

Skills to practice across the course

Read visual modelsCheck labels, units, and arrows before interpreting a map, diagram, or cycle.
Analyze sourcesIdentify the author’s claim, the evidence offered for it, and the reasoning that connects the two.
Design investigationsChoose a measurable dependent variable and an independent variable. State a comparison or control, hold other conditions constant, and replicate when possible.
Interpret dataDescribe direction and strength of a pattern, use values with units, and distinguish a relationship from evidence of causation.

Unit 1: The Living World: Ecosystems

6–8% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

1.1 Ecosystem interactions

Core ideaBiotic factors are living components. Abiotic factors include light, temperature, water, and soil. A population is one species in an area, a community is all its populations, and an ecosystem includes the community and its physical environment.
Apply itCompetition (−/−) occurs over limited resources, predation and parasitism (+/−) benefit one organism at another’s expense, mutualism (+/+) benefits both, and commensalism (+/0) benefits one without measurably affecting the other. Resource partitioning reduces competition.

1.2 Land biomes

Core ideaTemperature and precipitation shape vegetation. Tropical rainforests are warm and wet, deserts are dry, and savannas and temperate grasslands often experience seasonal drought and fire. Temperate forests have distinct seasons, boreal forests have cold winters and conifers, and tundra is cold with a short growing season.
Apply itClimate diagrams show monthly temperature and precipitation together. Compare both variables and their seasonal patterns when identifying a biome. Biome boundaries can shift as climates change.

1.3 Freshwater and marine biomes

Core ideaFreshwater includes flowing rivers and streams, standing lakes and ponds, and wetlands. Estuaries mix salt and fresh water, coral reefs typically occupy warm shallow seas, and open oceans vary in light and productivity with depth and nutrient supply.
Apply itSalinity, depth, temperature, flow, and light determine which organisms can live in an aquatic ecosystem. Wetlands and estuaries often provide nursery habitat and intercept runoff.

1.4 Cycling carbon

Core ideaPhotosynthesis draws CO₂ into organic matter. Feeding transfers it through food webs. Respiration and decomposition return carbon to the atmosphere or water. Carbon is also stored in soils, oceans, carbonate rocks, and fossil fuels.
Apply itFossil-fuel combustion moves long-stored carbon into the atmosphere. Deforestation reduces carbon uptake and can release carbon stored in vegetation and soil.

1.5 Cycling nitrogen

Core ideaFixation converts N₂ into ammonia or ammonium. Nitrification converts ammonium into nitrite and nitrate. Assimilation incorporates available nitrogen into organisms. Decomposition releases ammonium (ammonification). Denitrification returns nitrate to atmospheric N₂.
Apply itMost organisms cannot directly use atmospheric N₂. Fertilizer adds reactive nitrogen, and nitrate can leach into groundwater or wash into streams and contribute to eutrophication.

1.6 Cycling phosphorus

Core ideaWeathering releases phosphate from rock into soil and water. Plants take up phosphate, consumers acquire it through food, and waste and decomposition return it to the environment. Sedimentation can store it in rock over long periods.
Apply itPhosphorus has no major gas phase. It can limit freshwater productivity. Fertilizer and wastewater can add enough phosphorus to stimulate algal growth.

1.7 Cycling water

Core ideaEvaporation and transpiration move water into the atmosphere. Condensation forms droplets and precipitation returns water to Earth. Water infiltrates soil, percolates toward groundwater, runs off into waterways, or is stored in ice, soil, and surface water.
Apply itImpervious surfaces increase runoff and usually reduce infiltration and groundwater recharge. Plants help slow runoff and return water to the air through transpiration.

1.8 Measuring primary productivity

Core ideaGross primary productivity (GPP) is the rate at which producers capture energy. Net primary productivity (NPP) is what remains after producer respiration (R). NPP = GPP − R. Use units such as kJ/m²/year.
Apply itIf GPP is 2,000 and R is 800 kJ/m²/year, NPP is 1,200 kJ/m²/year. High light alone cannot ensure high productivity if water or nutrients are limiting.

1.9 Trophic roles

Core ideaProducers make organic matter. Primary consumers eat producers, secondary consumers eat primary consumers, and higher consumers feed above them. Detritivores break up dead matter and decomposers release nutrients as they break it down.
Apply itOmnivores can feed at more than one trophic level. The trophic level depends on the pathway being traced.

1.10 Energy transfer and the 10% rule

Core ideaRoughly 10% of energy at one trophic level is incorporated into biomass at the next, though actual efficiency varies. Organisms use energy for respiration and activity, release heat, and leave some material uneaten or undigested.
Apply itStarting with 10,000 kJ in producers, estimate 1,000 kJ in primary consumers and 100 kJ in secondary consumers. Energy flows in one direction while matter cycles.

1.11 Food webs and trophic cascades

Core ideaA food chain traces one feeding path. A food web shows several paths. Arrows point from the organism eaten toward the organism that receives its energy.
Apply itA decline in predators may increase herbivores and reduce producer biomass. A food web can buffer or redirect the effect through alternative prey or predators.

Unit 2: The Living World: Biodiversity

6–8% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

2.1 Types of biodiversity

Core ideaGenetic diversity is variation within a species. Species diversity reflects richness (how many species) and evenness (how evenly individuals are distributed). Ecosystem diversity is the variety of habitats or ecosystems in a region.
Apply itGenetic variation can help a population persist through a change. Functional overlap among species can support ecological processes after disturbance.

2.2 Benefits ecosystems provide

Core ideaProvisioning services supply food, water, and materials. Regulating services include flood moderation, pollination, and climate regulation. Cultural services include recreation. Supporting services include soil formation and nutrient cycling.
Apply itDraining a wetland may remove flood storage and water filtration at the same time as it reduces habitat.

2.3 Island size and isolation

Core ideaSpecies richness reflects a balance between immigration and extinction. Islands nearer a source population generally receive more colonists. Larger islands usually support larger populations and more habitats, which can lower extinction rates.
Apply itThe model also applies to forest patches and other habitat islands. Corridors can increase movement among isolated patches.

2.4 Tolerance limits

Core ideaAn organism has a range for factors such as temperature, pH, salinity, and dissolved oxygen. Performance is greatest near the optimum, falls in zones of stress, and survival fails outside the limits.
Apply itOn a tolerance curve, distinguish where a species survives from where it reproduces or grows best. Multiple environmental factors can limit its range.

2.5 Natural disturbance

Core ideaFires, storms, floods, droughts, and eruptions can change habitat, population sizes, and resource availability. Frequency, intensity, extent, and timing influence recovery.
Apply itResistance is how little an ecosystem changes during disturbance. Resilience is how well it recovers afterward. A fire-adapted ecosystem can recover from periodic burns but struggle if fires recur too frequently.

2.6 Adaptation and environmental change

Core ideaAn adaptation is a heritable trait that improves survival or reproduction in a particular environment. Over generations, individuals with advantageous inherited traits can leave more offspring, increasing those traits’ frequency.
Apply itA population facing a rapid change may shift its range or decline if reproduction, dispersal, and existing genetic variation are insufficient.

2.7 Succession and recovery

Core ideaPrimary succession starts where soil is absent, such as newly exposed rock. Pioneer organisms such as lichens help build soil. Secondary succession begins after a disturbance leaves soil intact, allowing faster recolonization.
Apply itEarly colonists alter habitat and influence later species. Disturbance and local conditions mean communities can follow different recovery paths.

Unit 3: Populations

10–15% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

3.1 Broad versus narrow niches

Core ideaGeneralists use a wide range of resources or conditions. Specialists use a narrower set and may perform better in a stable niche, while facing greater risk if a key resource disappears.
Apply itA generalist may colonize disturbed or urban habitat more easily. A specialist may depend on one prey, host, or microhabitat.

3.2 r- and K-selected strategies

Core idear-selected traits include early reproduction, many offspring, and limited parental investment. K-selected traits include later reproduction, fewer offspring, and greater investment in each. These are ends of a continuum.
Apply itRapid reproduction can help exploit short-lived resources. Slow reproduction can limit recovery after a population crash.

3.3 Survivorship curves

Core ideaType I shows high survival until later life. Type II shows about the same risk of death at each age. Type III shows high early mortality and better survival for individuals that make it past early life.
Apply itRead the axes before interpreting the graph. Humans are a common Type I example, many birds approximate Type II, and many oysters show Type III.

3.4 Carrying capacity and growth curves

Core ideaCarrying capacity (K) is the population a habitat can sustain with available resources. Exponential growth produces a J-shaped curve under favorable conditions. Logistic growth slows near K and produces an S-shaped curve.
Apply itK can shift with conditions such as drought or habitat loss. Overshoot can deplete resources and cause a dieback.

3.5 Limiting factors and population change

Core ideaPopulation change = births + immigration − deaths − emigration. Competition and disease are often density dependent. Storms and freezes are generally density independent.
Apply itGrowth over an interval (%) = population change ÷ starting population × 100. A rise from 500 to 550 is 10% growth over that interval.

3.6 Reading age structure

Core ideaA broad base indicates many young people and usually future growth. A more rectangular shape can indicate slower growth or stability. A narrow base can indicate future decline.
Apply itPopulation momentum occurs when a large cohort is about to reach reproductive age. Population can keep rising after fertility drops.

3.7 Fertility and replacement

Core ideaTotal fertility rate (TFR) is the average number of children a woman is expected to have under current age-specific birth rates. Replacement fertility is about 2.1 in many low-mortality populations and can be higher where child mortality is higher.
Apply itEducation, reproductive healthcare, child survival, urbanization, and economic conditions can affect fertility.

3.8 Human population rates

Core ideaA crude birth or death rate is usually expressed per 1,000 people per year. Natural growth rate (%) ≈ (crude birth rate − crude death rate) ÷ 10. Include net migration for overall growth.
Apply itRule of 70: doubling time in years ≈ 70 ÷ annual growth rate in percent, if a positive rate stays approximately constant.

3.9 Demographic transition

Core ideaStage 1 has high birth and death rates. Stage 2 has falling death rates while births remain high. Stage 3 has falling birth rates. Stage 4 has low birth and death rates. Some models add a decline stage.
Apply itPopulation grows fastest when the gap between birth and death rates is largest. Cleaner water and better healthcare often lower deaths before economic and social changes reduce births.

Unit 4: Earth Systems and Resources

10–15% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

4.1 Moving tectonic plates

Core ideaDivergent boundaries create new crust. Convergent boundaries involve subduction or continental collision. Transform boundaries slide sideways and commonly produce earthquakes.
Apply itSubduction can form trenches and volcanic arcs. A sudden vertical displacement of the seafloor can produce a tsunami.

4.2 Soil formation and loss

Core ideaParent material, climate, organisms, relief, and time influence soil formation. Weathering breaks material down. Erosion moves it. A typical profile can include O organic material, A topsoil, B subsoil, C parent material, and R bedrock.
Apply itRemoving plant cover exposes topsoil to wind and runoff. Roots, mulch, and contour practices slow erosion.

4.3 Soil texture, water, and nutrients

Core ideaSand has large particles and often drains quickly. Clay has small particles, often retains more water and nutrients, and drains more slowly. Loam mixes sand, silt, and clay. Organic matter improves soil structure and nutrient retention.
Apply itPorosity is the fraction of space available to hold water or air. Permeability is how easily water can move through connected pores. Soil pH affects nutrient availability.

4.4 Atmospheric layers

Core ideaDry air is mostly nitrogen (~78%) and oxygen (~21%). Most weather occurs in the troposphere. Stratospheric ozone absorbs much ultraviolet radiation, the mesosphere lies above it, and the thermosphere is higher still.
Apply itTemperature generally falls with altitude in the troposphere and rises through much of the stratosphere as ozone absorbs UV radiation.

4.5 Global winds and rainfall belts

Core ideaUneven solar heating establishes circulation cells. Warm moist air rises near the equator, cools, and drops rain. Drier air sinks near 30° latitude. Earth’s rotation deflects moving air right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern Hemisphere.
Apply itHadley, Ferrel, and polar cells help explain trade winds, westerlies, and polar easterlies. Follow a parcel up or down to predict cooling or warming.

4.6 Watershed boundaries

Core ideaA watershed is land that drains to a common outlet. Topography defines divides. Slope, soil, vegetation, and pavement control infiltration, runoff timing, and the pollutants carried into streams.
Apply itAn upstream change can affect downstream organisms. Comparing upstream and downstream samples can help investigate a potential pollution source.

4.7 Solar angle and seasons

Core ideaEarth’s axial tilt changes sunlight angle and day length seasonally. Direct rays concentrate energy over a smaller area. A hemisphere tilted toward the Sun generally receives longer days and more direct sunlight.
Apply itThe hemispheres have opposite seasons. Earth–Sun distance does not account for that pattern.

4.8 Geography and climate

Core ideaLarge water bodies moderate nearby temperatures. Ocean currents redistribute heat. Rising air cools and drops rain over a mountain’s windward side, while descending air warms and dries on the leeward side.
Apply itThe dry leeward region is a rain shadow. Higher elevations generally have cooler air.

4.9 El Niño and La Niña

Core ideaEl Niño usually weakens Pacific trade winds and reduces cool, nutrient-rich upwelling off western South America as warmer surface water shifts east. La Niña typically strengthens those conditions in the opposite direction.
Apply itLower upwelling can reduce marine productivity and fishery yields. Weather effects vary by region and season.

Unit 5: Land and Water Use

10–15% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

5.1 Shared resources

Core ideaWhen users capture their own benefit while sharing the environmental cost, an unmanaged resource such as a fishery can be overused.
Apply itCatch limits, enforceable access rules, or community management can align private use with long-term resource renewal.

5.2 Effects of clearcutting

Core ideaClearcutting removes most or all trees in a stand. It can reduce habitat and carbon storage and increase erosion, runoff, sediment in streams, and stream temperatures.
Apply itRetaining stream buffers shades waterways and intercepts sediment. Selective cutting limits canopy removal but may still fragment habitat.

5.3 Green Revolution trade-offs

Core ideaHigher-yield crop varieties, irrigation, fertilizer, pesticides, and mechanization increased food production. Productive systems can depend on more external inputs.
Apply itHigher yield can reduce land needed per unit of food, while intensive irrigation or fertilizer use can deplete water or increase pollution.

5.4 Farming impacts

Core ideaTilling exposes soil to erosion. Monocultures can increase pest vulnerability. Excess fertilizer promotes nutrient pollution, pesticides can harm nontarget species, and intensive grazing can compact soil and remove plant cover.
Apply itConnect each practice to a mechanism and outcome. For example, reduced soil cover increases erosion, which adds sediment to streams.

5.5 Irrigation methods

Core ideaFlood irrigation spreads water over a field. Furrows channel water between rows. Sprinklers spray it overhead. Drip delivers it near roots and is typically most efficient, although installation and maintenance cost more.
Apply itWaterlogging limits root oxygen. Where evaporation is high or drainage is poor, salts accumulate and reduce crop growth.

5.6 Pest control and resistance

Core ideaChemical controls can work quickly but may affect nontarget organisms. Repeated use selects for resistant pests. Biological controls rely on natural enemies. Mechanical and cultural methods include traps, exclusion, and rotation.
Apply itPesticide resistance spreads because resistant individuals survive and reproduce. Individual pests do not become resistant in response to a spray.

5.7 Raising livestock

Core ideaFeedlots concentrate animals, increasing production efficiency and creating concentrated manure and wastewater. Pasture distributes manure but requires more land and can degrade soils if overgrazed.
Apply itEnergy losses between trophic levels make livestock production resource intensive. Ruminants also emit methane from digestion.

5.8 Fishing pressures

Core ideaOverfishing removes individuals faster than a population replaces them. Bottom trawling can damage habitat, and bycatch kills nontarget animals. Populations can decline before a fishery becomes unprofitable.
Apply itQuotas, gear rules, catch-size limits, and protected areas can reduce impacts. Effective limits depend on monitoring and enforcement.

5.9 Mining and reclamation

Core ideaSurface mining disturbs large areas. Subsurface mining can cause subsidence and leaves waste. Acid mine drainage occurs when exposed sulfide minerals react with air and water, creating acidic drainage that can mobilize metals.
Apply itReclamation can restore contours, replace topsoil, and replant vegetation. Managing tailings and drainage reduces ongoing pollution.

5.10 Urbanization and sprawl

Core ideaImpervious surfaces increase stormwater runoff, flood peaks, and pollutant transport. Dark surfaces and reduced vegetation produce urban heat islands. Sprawl fragments habitat and often increases car use.
Apply itTrees provide shade and cooling. Compact development and public transport can reduce land and fuel demand.

5.11 Ecological footprints

Core ideaAn ecological footprint estimates the biologically productive area needed to supply consumption and absorb associated waste under current practices. Biocapacity estimates what ecosystems can supply.
Apply itCompare per-capita and total footprints separately. Total footprint depends on both population and consumption per person.

5.12 Sustainability and resource renewal

Core ideaSustainable use meets current needs while maintaining future capacity. Harvest of a renewable resource should stay within its regeneration rate, and waste inputs should stay within the system’s ability to process them.
Apply itChoose indicators that can be monitored, such as fish biomass, groundwater levels, or soil organic matter, and compare them over time.

5.13 Reducing urban runoff

Core ideaRain gardens and bioswales slow and filter runoff. Permeable paving supports infiltration. Green roofs intercept rain, and detention basins slow discharge and reduce flood peaks.
Apply itThese measures target different outcomes. For example, a detention basin can reduce peak flow even if it does not substantially reduce total runoff.

5.14 Integrated pest management

Core ideaIPM combines monitoring, action thresholds, prevention, biological and physical controls, and targeted pesticide use when needed.
Apply itRotate crops and protect predators, then use a selective treatment only when monitoring shows the economic or damage threshold has been reached.

5.15 Maintaining productive soil

Core ideaCover crops and crop rotation protect soil and cycle nutrients. Contour plowing, terraces, windbreaks, and conservation tillage reduce erosion. Intercropping and agroforestry diversify production.
Apply itOn a slope, terraces shorten the effective slope and slow runoff. Cover crops protect exposed soil and can reduce nutrient loss.

5.16 Aquaculture benefits and costs

Core ideaAquaculture raises aquatic organisms for food and can ease pressure on wild fisheries. Crowding may increase disease, escaped stocks may affect wild populations, and waste or feed inputs can degrade water quality.
Apply itRecirculating systems reduce discharge and escapes but can use more energy. Well-managed filter-feeding shellfish may remove suspended particles.

5.17 Sustainable forest management

Core ideaSelective logging, replanting, protecting buffers and old-growth areas, and maintaining a mix of species and ages can support continued timber production and ecosystem services.
Apply itPrescribed burns can reduce fuel loads in fire-adapted forests. Forest restoration also depends on suitable species and long-term monitoring.

Unit 6: Energy Resources and Consumption

10–15% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

6.1 Renewable and finite energy

Core ideaRenewable sources replenish on human time scales if managed appropriately. Fossil fuels and uranium are finite resources. Renewable does not imply zero environmental impact.
Apply itCompare sources by availability, reliability, emissions, land use, and effects of extraction and disposal.

6.2 Comparing energy use

Core ideaPower is energy per unit time. Energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (hours). Total and per-capita consumption can show different patterns. Energy sources and uses depend on access, infrastructure, and economics.
Apply itA 1.5 kW heater used for 4 h draws 6 kWh. At $0.15/kWh, it costs $0.90 to operate.

6.3 Fuel uses and emissions

Core ideaCoal is mainly used for electricity and industry, petroleum products for transportation, and natural gas for heating and electricity. Fossil fuels release CO₂ when burned. Coal also commonly releases more sulfur dioxide and particulates than natural gas.
Apply itCompare emissions per unit of useful energy. Include methane leakage when evaluating the climate impact of natural gas.

6.4 Distribution of energy resources

Core ideaGeologic history shapes where coal, petroleum, gas, and uranium occur. Sunlight, winds, rivers, and geothermal heat vary geographically. A reserve is the part of a known resource that is recoverable under current conditions.
Apply itPractical energy potential also depends on access to land, transmission, water, and storage or backup.

6.5 Fossil-fuel extraction and combustion

Core ideaCoal mining can remove habitat and create acid drainage. Petroleum can spill. Hydraulic fracturing uses water and generates wastewater. Combustion makes CO₂ and may make NOₓ, SO₂, particulate matter, or mercury emissions.
Apply itNatural gas generally releases less CO₂ when combusted per unit of energy than coal, but methane leaks can reduce that advantage.

6.6 Nuclear fission

Core ideaFission splits heavy nuclei and releases heat. Heat makes steam to turn a turbine. Control rods absorb neutrons to regulate the chain reaction. Nuclear plants have low operational CO₂ emissions.
Apply itConsider long-lived radioactive waste, cooling-water impacts, mining, accidents, and decommissioning when comparing costs and benefits.

6.7 Biomass fuels

Core ideaWood, crop residues, landfill gas, and biofuels supply energy from organic material. Burning biomass can produce CO₂ and particles. Its net climate impact depends on regrowth, land-use changes, and fuel production.
Apply itCapturing methane from decomposing waste can supply fuel. Growing crops for fuel can compete with food production or habitat.

6.8 Solar technologies

Core ideaPassive solar building design uses orientation and materials to capture heat. Photovoltaic cells convert light to electricity. Solar thermal systems capture heat, and some use it to generate electricity.
Apply itOutput varies with daylight, cloud cover, and location. Storage, transmission, or complementary power can help manage variability.

6.9 Hydroelectricity

Core ideaMoving water turns turbines. Dam reservoirs can provide electricity, storage, and flood control, but also flood habitat, alter flow, trap sediments, and block fish migration.
Apply itA fish passage can reduce barriers for some species. Maintaining downstream flow helps protect habitat.

6.10 Geothermal energy

Core ideaGeothermal plants use heat from underground to generate electricity or supply direct heat. Ground-source heat pumps use relatively stable shallow ground temperatures for heating and cooling.
Apply itPower generation is site dependent. Potential impacts include water use, mineral-bearing fluids, and induced seismicity in some systems.

6.11 Hydrogen fuel cells

Core ideaA fuel cell combines hydrogen and oxygen electrochemically to produce electricity, water, and heat. Hydrogen is an energy carrier that must be produced first.
Apply itLife-cycle emissions depend on the hydrogen source and energy used to produce it, even though a hydrogen fuel cell emits water at the point of use.

6.12 Wind power

Core ideaTurbines convert wind’s kinetic energy into electricity without fuel combustion. Output varies with the wind. Siting affects transmission costs, wildlife impacts, and noise.
Apply itWind farms can share land with agriculture. Careful siting and temporary shutdowns during high-risk periods can reduce bird and bat deaths.

6.13 Conservation and efficiency

Core ideaConservation reduces demand, such as using an appliance less often. Efficiency provides the same service with less energy, such as using LEDs or improving insulation.
Apply itEfficiency (%) = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100. Estimate savings with the difference in power multiplied by hours of use.

Unit 7: Atmospheric Pollution

7–10% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

7.1 Primary and secondary air pollutants

Core ideaPrimary pollutants are emitted directly, such as CO, SO₂, and some particulates. Secondary pollutants form in the atmosphere, such as ground-level ozone and many sulfate and nitrate particles.
Apply itIncomplete combustion produces CO. High-temperature combustion produces NOₓ. Sulfur in fuels contributes to SO₂ emissions.

7.2 How photochemical smog forms

Core ideaSunlight drives reactions involving NOₓ and volatile organic compounds (VOCs), producing ground-level ozone and other secondary pollutants. Hot, sunny, stagnant conditions can worsen episodes.
Apply itGround-level ozone irritates airways and damages plant tissues. Cutting precursor emissions from vehicles and industry can reduce smog, although the local chemistry affects the response.

7.3 Temperature inversions

Core ideaNormally, surface-heated air can rise and mix. During an inversion, a warmer layer above cooler surface air suppresses vertical mixing and traps pollutants near ground level.
Apply itAn inversion can raise pollutant concentration without raising the emission rate. Valleys and calm conditions can make trapping more pronounced.

7.4 Atmospheric CO₂ and fine particles

Core ideaRespiration, decomposition, and volcanoes release CO₂ naturally. Fossil fuel use and land-use change add more. Natural particles include windblown dust, sea salt, wildfire smoke, and volcanic ash. Human sources include vehicles, power plants, and industrial activity. Fine PM₂.₅ penetrates deeper into lungs than larger particles.
Apply itRising CO₂ drives long-term warming. Fine particles can cause direct respiratory and cardiovascular harm, and some aerosols affect how much sunlight reaches the surface.

7.5 Indoor pollution and exposure

Core ideaRadon can enter buildings from soil and rock. Incomplete combustion can release CO. Smoke, VOCs, mold, and disturbed asbestos are other indoor hazards. Risk depends on concentration, exposure duration, and ventilation.
Apply itMatch control to source: test and mitigate radon, vent combustion, use CO alarms, and address moisture that supports mold growth.

7.6 Controlling emissions

Core ideaCatalytic converters reduce vehicle CO, NOₓ, and unburned hydrocarbons. Scrubbers can remove SO₂. Filters and electrostatic precipitators capture particles. Cleaner fuels and transit reduce emissions at the source.
Apply itSelect a technology that targets the pollutant. In the U.S., the Clean Air Act provides a framework for air-quality standards and emissions controls.

7.7 Acid deposition

Core ideaAtmospheric reactions convert SO₂ and NOₓ into sulfuric and nitric acids, which reach surfaces through wet or dry deposition. Impacts include acidified lakes, nutrient loss from soils, dissolved aluminum, and material damage.
Apply itLimestone-rich watersheds can buffer some acidity. Reducing SO₂ and NOₓ addresses the cause.

7.8 Noise exposure

Core ideaRoad traffic, construction, aircraft, and industry produce noise. Chronic exposure can disrupt sleep and communication, cause stress, and damage hearing at high levels. Underwater noise can interfere with navigation and communication in marine animals.
Apply itBarriers, quieter equipment, scheduling, and buffers reduce exposure. The decibel scale is logarithmic.

Unit 8: Aquatic and Terrestrial Pollution

7–10% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

8.1 Point and nonpoint pollution

Core ideaA point source is a specific discharge location such as a pipe. Nonpoint pollution comes from diffuse areas, such as stormwater washing nutrients off many fields or roads.
Apply itTreating a discharge at its outlet can control a point source. Vegetated buffers and better land management help reduce nonpoint loads.

8.2 Aquatic ecosystem stress

Core ideaSediment increases turbidity and can smother habitat. Microbes decomposing organic waste consume oxygen. High biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) can lead to low dissolved oxygen (DO). Oil and toxic metals can harm organisms directly.
Apply itAn oxygen sag curve shows a downstream drop in DO and recovery as oxygen is replenished. Compare BOD, temperature, and upstream values before deciding what caused the drop.

8.3 Hormone disruption

Core ideaEndocrine disruptors can mimic or block hormones or change their production, affecting development and reproduction. Examples include some pesticides and industrial compounds.
Apply itEffects can depend on dose and timing. A sensitive developmental stage can be more vulnerable than adulthood.

8.4 Wetland and mangrove loss

Core ideaThese habitats filter water, store carbon, provide nursery habitat, and reduce flood or storm damage. Draining, dredging, development, and pollution can degrade those services.
Apply itRestoring tidal flow or protecting buffers supports recovery. Mangroves can reduce coastal wave energy and trap sediment.

8.5 Nutrients, blooms, and hypoxia

Core ideaAdded nitrogen or phosphorus can stimulate algal blooms. When algae die, microbes consume oxygen during decomposition, causing hypoxia or anoxia and sometimes fish kills. Some blooms also make toxins.
Apply itPrevent excess fertilizer and improve wastewater nutrient removal. The key chain is added nutrients → more algae → more decomposition → less DO.

8.6 Heat as a pollutant

Core ideaWarm discharges lower water’s oxygen solubility and can raise organisms’ metabolic oxygen demand. Sensitive species can decline as warmer-tolerant species become more common.
Apply itCooling towers or cooling ponds can reduce discharge temperature. Shaded stream buffers also limit warming.

8.7 Persistent organic pollutants

Core ideaPOPs break down slowly, can travel far, and often accumulate in fatty tissue. Some pesticides and industrial chemicals, such as DDT and PCBs, are examples.
Apply itAn exposure can continue after production stops because contaminated soils or sediments can remain a source.

8.8 Accumulation within organisms and food webs

Core ideaBioaccumulation is buildup in an individual when uptake outpaces elimination. Biomagnification is increasing concentration across trophic levels for some persistent contaminants.
Apply itPredatory fish can have higher methylmercury concentrations than their prey. Compare concentration in the same units before drawing conclusions.

8.9 Handling solid waste

Core ideaSanitary landfills use liners and leachate collection, but require monitoring and generate methane. Incineration reduces waste volume and can recover energy, but produces emissions and ash.
Apply itRCRA governs ongoing solid and hazardous waste management. CERCLA (Superfund) addresses cleanup of contaminated sites.

8.10 Reducing the waste stream

Core ideaSource reduction avoids making waste. Reuse extends product life. Recycling recovers material, and composting diverts organic matter into a useful soil amendment.
Apply itA waste strategy can reduce extraction and disposal, but collection, contamination, and processing influence its success.

8.11 Treating wastewater

Core ideaPrimary treatment removes solids physically. Secondary treatment uses microbes to degrade organic matter. Advanced treatment can remove nutrients and other contaminants. Disinfection controls pathogens.
Apply itLower BOD reduces downstream oxygen stress. The Clean Water Act regulates pollutant discharges to surface waters, while the Safe Drinking Water Act focuses on public drinking-water quality.

8.12 LD₅₀ and toxicity comparisons

Core ideaLD₅₀ is the dose that kills 50% of a test population under specific conditions, often reported in mg/kg. A lower LD₅₀ indicates greater acute toxicity under comparable conditions.
Apply itDose (mg/kg) = mass of substance received (mg) ÷ body mass (kg). LD₅₀ alone does not show chronic effects or susceptibility across species.

8.13 Interpreting dose–response graphs

Core ideaThe x-axis shows dose and the y-axis shows a measured response. Depending on the graph, it can show mortality, a sublethal effect, or the percentage of a population responding.
Apply itTo estimate LD₅₀, find 50% mortality on the y-axis and read the corresponding dose. Control groups help distinguish treatment effects from background responses.

8.14 Pollution and health risks

Core ideaExposure occurs through inhalation, ingestion, or contact. Acute exposure is short term. Chronic exposure repeats or persists. A hazard is the potential to cause harm, while risk depends on hazard and exposure.
Apply itThe Delaney Clause prohibits approving food additives found to cause cancer in humans or animals under its scope. Use exposure and dose when explaining why two groups may face different risks.

8.15 Infectious diseases and the environment

Core ideaPathogens include disease-causing viruses, bacteria, and parasites. Transmission can be direct, waterborne, or vector-borne. Unsafe water can spread cholera, and mosquitoes can transmit malaria.
Apply itWater treatment and sanitation address waterborne exposure. Warming can shift the geographic range of some disease vectors. Vector control and habitat management can reduce contact with them.

Unit 9: Global Change

15–20% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

9.1 Stratospheric ozone loss

Core ideaStratospheric ozone absorbs UV radiation. UV breaks down long-lived compounds such as CFCs, releasing chlorine radicals that catalytically destroy ozone. Polar conditions can accelerate depletion.
Apply itThinner ozone increases UV-B exposure, with consequences for skin, eyes, and organisms such as phytoplankton.

9.2 Protecting the ozone layer

Core ideaThe Montreal Protocol phases out major ozone-depleting substances. Recovery is gradual because some compounds persist in the atmosphere for decades.
Apply itReplacing CFCs with compounds that do not destroy ozone helps, but some substitutes are strong greenhouse gases. Evaluate both effects.

9.3 Greenhouse warming

Core ideaEarth absorbs solar energy and emits infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some of that infrared radiation. The natural greenhouse effect keeps the surface warmer, while rising concentrations strengthen it.
Apply itMajor greenhouse gases include water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide. Ozone depletion and greenhouse warming have different mechanisms.

9.4 Why greenhouse gases increase

Core ideaFossil-fuel use, cement production, and land clearing add CO₂. Ruminants, landfills, rice cultivation, and fossil fuel operations emit methane. Fertilized soils can emit nitrous oxide.
Apply itGlobal warming potential compares a gas’s warming effect with CO₂ over a specified time. The Kyoto Protocol set greenhouse-gas targets for participating industrialized countries.

9.5 Climate trends and responses

Core ideaEvidence includes rising temperatures, ice loss, and sea-level rise. Warmer water expands and melting land ice adds water, raising sea level. Climate change also shifts rainfall, habitat ranges, and the risk of some extreme events.
Apply itMitigation limits the causes of warming, such as reducing emissions. Adaptation reduces harm from effects, such as improving flood protection or managing heat risk.

9.6 Effects of warmer oceans

Core ideaHeat stress can cause corals to expel their symbiotic algae (bleaching). Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen and may strengthen stratification, reducing vertical mixing of oxygen and nutrients.
Apply itBleaching can be reversible if stress subsides, but prolonged or repeated bleaching can increase mortality. Marine species may shift toward cooler water.

9.7 Ocean acidification chemistry

Core ideaDissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions. Rising hydrogen-ion concentration lowers pH and reduces available carbonate ions that many organisms use to build calcium carbonate structures.
Apply itLower pH can make shell and skeleton formation more difficult for corals, mollusks, and some plankton. A one-unit pH decrease means ten times the hydrogen-ion concentration.

9.8 Invasive species

Core ideaAn invasive species is introduced outside its native range and causes harm. It can spread when predators or pathogens are scarce, resources are available, and it reproduces or disperses successfully.
Apply itPrevent introductions through inspection and cleaning. Early detection is often easier than controlling an established population. Consider risks to nontarget species when selecting controls.

9.9 Endangerment and protection

Core ideaSmall populations, narrow ranges, specialized niches, and slow reproduction increase extinction risk. In the U.S., the Endangered Species Act protects listed species and supports recovery. CITES restricts international trade in listed wildlife and plants.
Apply itRecovery tools include protecting critical habitat, reconnecting populations, restoring habitat, and carefully managed captive breeding or reintroduction.

9.10 Human pressures on biodiversity

Core ideaHabitat loss and fragmentation, invasive species, pollution, climate change, and overharvesting can reduce biodiversity. Fragmentation creates edge effects and can reduce gene flow.
Apply itProtected areas, corridors, restoration, and sustainable harvest target different pressures. Evaluate success by tracking species abundance, richness, or habitat quality over time.

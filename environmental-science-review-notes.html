AP Environmental Science Quick Review Notes

Based on the College Board course framework for 2026–27.

Skills to practice across the course

Analyze sourcesIdentify the author’s claim, the evidence offered for it, and the reasoning that connects the two.
Design investigationsChoose a measurable dependent variable and an independent variable. State a comparison or control, hold other conditions constant, and replicate when possible.

Unit 1: The Living World: Ecosystems

6–8% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

1.1 Ecosystem interactions

Living and physical components
  • Biotic factors: living components
  • Abiotic factors: light, temperature, water, and soil
  • Population: one species in an area
  • Community: all populations in that area
  • Ecosystem: the community and its physical environment
Species interactions
  • Competition (−/−): both organisms compete for limited resources
  • Predation and parasitism (+/−): one organism benefits at the other’s expense
  • Mutualism (+/+): both benefit
  • Commensalism (+/0): one benefits and the other is unaffected
  • Resource partitioning reduces competition

1.2 Land biomes

Climate and vegetation
  • Tropical rainforest: warm and wet
  • Desert: dry
  • Savanna and temperate grassland: often seasonal drought and fire
  • Temperate forest: distinct seasons
  • Boreal forest: cold winters and conifers
  • Tundra: cold with a short growing season
Reading a climate diagram
  • Climate diagrams show monthly temperature and precipitation together.
  • Compare both variables and their seasonal patterns when identifying a biome.
  • Biome boundaries can shift as climates change.

1.3 Freshwater and marine biomes

Freshwater and marine habitats
  • Freshwater: rivers and streams flow, lakes and ponds hold standing water, and wetlands have saturated soils
  • Estuary: freshwater and saltwater mix
  • Coral reef: typically warm, shallow seas
  • Open ocean: light and productivity vary with depth and nutrient supply
What shapes aquatic life
  • Salinity, depth, temperature, flow, and light determine which organisms can live in an aquatic ecosystem.
  • Wetlands and estuaries often provide nursery habitat and intercept runoff.

1.4 Cycling carbon

Carbon stores and transfers
  • Photosynthesis: CO₂ → organic matter
  • Feeding moves carbon through food webs
  • Respiration and decomposition return carbon to air or water
  • Long-term stores include soils, oceans, carbonate rocks, and fossil fuels
Combustion and forest loss
  • Fossil-fuel combustion moves long-stored carbon into the atmosphere.
  • Deforestation reduces carbon uptake and can release carbon stored in vegetation and soil.

1.5 Cycling nitrogen

Nitrogen transformations
  • Fixation: N₂ → ammonia or ammonium
  • Nitrification: ammonium → nitrite → nitrate
  • Assimilation: organisms take up available nitrogen
  • Ammonification: decomposition releases ammonium
  • Denitrification: nitrate → atmospheric N₂
Fertilizer and runoff
  • Most organisms cannot directly use atmospheric N₂.
  • Fertilizer adds reactive nitrogen, and nitrate can leach into groundwater or wash into streams and contribute to eutrophication.

1.6 Cycling phosphorus

Phosphate pathways
  • Weathering releases phosphate from rock
  • Plants take up phosphate, and consumers obtain it through food
  • Waste and decomposition return phosphate to soil and water
  • Sedimentation can lock phosphorus into rock long term
Freshwater nutrient limits
  • Phosphorus has no major gas phase.
  • It can limit freshwater productivity.
  • Fertilizer and wastewater can add enough phosphorus to stimulate algal growth.

1.7 Cycling water

Water movement and storage
  • Evaporation and transpiration move water into the air
  • Condensation forms droplets, and precipitation returns water to Earth
  • Infiltration and percolation move water into soil and toward groundwater
  • Runoff delivers water to waterways
  • Ice, soil, and surface water store water
Pavement and groundwater recharge
  • Impervious surfaces increase runoff and usually reduce infiltration and groundwater recharge.
  • Plants help slow runoff and return water to the air through transpiration.

1.8 Measuring primary productivity

Productivity and respiration
  • GPP: rate at which producers capture energy
  • R: energy used by producers in respiration
  • NPP = GPP − R
  • Example units: kJ/m²/year
Worked NPP example
  • 2,000 − 800 = 1,200 kJ/m²/year
  • High light may have little effect if water or nutrients are limiting

1.9 Trophic roles

Producers, consumers, and decomposers
  • Producers make organic matter
  • Primary consumers eat producers
  • Secondary consumers eat primary consumers
  • Higher consumers feed at higher levels
  • Detritivores break up dead matter, and decomposers release nutrients as they break it down
Omnivores in food webs
  • Omnivores can feed at more than one trophic level.
  • The trophic level depends on the pathway being traced.

1.10 Energy transfer and the 10% rule

Transfer efficiency
  • Roughly 10% of energy at one trophic level is incorporated into biomass at the next, though actual efficiency varies.
  • Organisms use energy for respiration and activity, release heat, and leave some material uneaten or undigested.
Energy pyramid example
  • 10,000 kJ in producers → 1,000 kJ in primary consumers → 100 kJ in secondary consumers
  • Energy flows in one direction while matter cycles

1.11 Food webs and trophic cascades

Reading food-web arrows
  • A food chain traces one feeding path.
  • A food web shows several paths.
  • Arrows point from the organism eaten toward the organism that receives its energy.
What happens when predators decline
  • A decline in predators may increase herbivores and reduce producer biomass.
  • A food web can buffer or redirect the effect through alternative prey or predators.

Unit 2: The Living World: Biodiversity

6–8% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

2.1 Types of biodiversity

Levels of diversity
  • Genetic diversity: variation within a species
  • Species richness: how many species are present
  • Species evenness: how evenly individuals are distributed across species
  • Ecosystem diversity: variety of habitats or ecosystems
Why variation matters
  • Genetic variation can help a population persist through a change.
  • Functional overlap among species can support ecological processes after disturbance.

2.2 Benefits ecosystems provide

Four types of ecosystem services
  • Provisioning: food, water, and materials
  • Regulating: flood moderation, pollination, and climate regulation
  • Cultural: recreation
  • Supporting: soil formation and nutrient cycling
What wetland drainage removes
  • Draining a wetland may remove flood storage and water filtration at the same time as it reduces habitat.

2.3 Island size and isolation

Immigration and extinction
  • Species richness reflects a balance between immigration and extinction.
  • Islands nearer a source population generally receive more colonists.
  • Larger islands usually support larger populations and more habitats, which can lower extinction rates.
Habitat patches and corridors
  • The model also applies to forest patches and other habitat islands.
  • Corridors can increase movement among isolated patches.

2.4 Tolerance limits

Optimum, stress, and survival
  • Tolerance factors: temperature, pH, salinity, and dissolved oxygen
  • Optimum: highest performance
  • Stress zone: reduced performance
  • Outside tolerance limits: survival fails
Reading a tolerance curve
  • On a tolerance curve, distinguish where a species survives from where it reproduces or grows best.
  • Multiple environmental factors can limit its range.

2.5 Natural disturbance

Disturbance and recovery
  • Fires, storms, floods, droughts, and eruptions can change habitat, population sizes, and resource availability.
  • Frequency, intensity, extent, and timing influence recovery.
Resistance versus resilience
  • Resistance is how little an ecosystem changes during disturbance.
  • Resilience is how well it recovers afterward.
  • A fire-adapted ecosystem can recover from periodic burns but struggle if fires recur too frequently.

2.6 Adaptation and environmental change

Heritable traits and selection
  • An adaptation is a heritable trait that improves survival or reproduction in a particular environment.
  • Over generations, individuals with advantageous inherited traits can leave more offspring, increasing those traits’ frequency.
When conditions change quickly
  • A population facing a rapid change may shift its range or decline if reproduction, dispersal, and existing genetic variation are insufficient.

2.7 Succession and recovery

Primary versus secondary succession
  • Primary succession starts where soil is absent, such as newly exposed rock
  • Pioneers such as lichens help build soil
  • Secondary succession starts after disturbance leaves soil intact, so recolonization is usually faster
How early colonists shape recovery
  • Early colonists alter habitat and influence later species.
  • Disturbance and local conditions mean communities can follow different recovery paths.

Unit 3: Populations

10–15% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

3.1 Broad versus narrow niches

Niche breadth
  • Generalists use a wide range of resources or conditions.
  • Specialists use a narrower set and may perform better in a stable niche, while facing greater risk if a key resource disappears.
Who handles disturbance better
  • A generalist may colonize disturbed or urban habitat more easily.
  • A specialist may depend on one prey, host, or microhabitat.

3.2 r- and K-selected strategies

Reproductive strategies
  • r-selected: early reproduction, many offspring, and limited parental investment
  • K-selected: later reproduction, fewer offspring, and greater investment per offspring
  • These strategies fall along a continuum
Growth and recovery trade-offs
  • Rapid reproduction can help exploit short-lived resources.
  • Slow reproduction can limit recovery after a population crash.

3.3 Survivorship curves

Three survival patterns
  • Type I: high survival until later life
  • Type II: similar risk of death at any age
  • Type III: high early mortality, with better survival for those that reach later life
Species examples
  • Check the axes before interpreting the graph
  • Type I: humans
  • Type II: many birds
  • Type III: many oysters

3.4 Carrying capacity and growth curves

Growth curves and carrying capacity
  • Carrying capacity (K) is the population a habitat can sustain with available resources.
  • Exponential growth produces a J-shaped curve under favorable conditions.
  • Logistic growth slows near K and produces an S-shaped curve.
Overshoot and dieback
  • K can shift with conditions such as drought or habitat loss.
  • Overshoot can deplete resources and cause a dieback.

3.5 Limiting factors and population change

Population gains and losses
  • Population change = births + immigration − deaths − emigration
  • Density-dependent factors: competition and disease
  • Density-independent factors: storms and freezes
Percent growth example
  • Growth (%) = population change ÷ starting population × 100
  • Example: (550 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = 10%

3.6 Reading age structure

Reading an age pyramid
  • Broad base: many young people, usually future growth
  • Rectangular shape: slower growth or stability
  • Narrow base: possible future decline
Population momentum
  • Population momentum occurs when a large cohort is about to reach reproductive age.
  • Population can keep rising after fertility drops.

3.7 Fertility and replacement

Fertility and replacement
  • Total fertility rate (TFR) is the average number of children a woman is expected to have under current age-specific birth rates.
  • Replacement fertility is about 2.1 in many low-mortality populations and can be higher where child mortality is higher.
Factors that affect fertility
  • Education, reproductive healthcare, child survival, urbanization, and economic conditions can affect fertility.

3.8 Human population rates

Birth, death, and migration rates
  • Crude birth or death rate: events per 1,000 people per year
  • Natural growth (%) ≈ (crude birth rate − crude death rate) ÷ 10
  • Include net migration for overall growth
Rule of 70
  • Doubling time (years) ≈ 70 ÷ annual growth rate (%)
  • Assumes a positive, approximately constant growth rate

3.9 Demographic transition

Stages of demographic transition
  • Stage 1: high birth and death rates
  • Stage 2: death rate declines while births stay high
  • Stage 3: birth rate declines
  • Stage 4: low birth and death rates
  • Some models include a final decline stage
When growth peaks
  • Population grows fastest when the gap between birth and death rates is largest.
  • Cleaner water and better healthcare often lower deaths before economic and social changes reduce births.

Unit 4: Earth Systems and Resources

10–15% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

4.1 Moving tectonic plates

Plate boundaries
  • Divergent boundaries create new crust.
  • Convergent boundaries involve subduction or continental collision.
  • Transform boundaries slide sideways and commonly produce earthquakes.
Trenches, volcanoes, and tsunamis
  • Subduction can form trenches and volcanic arcs.
  • A sudden vertical displacement of the seafloor can produce a tsunami.

4.2 Soil formation and loss

Soil development and horizons
  • Soil formation depends on parent material, climate, organisms, relief, and time
  • Weathering breaks material down. Erosion moves it
  • O organic matter → A topsoil → B subsoil → C parent material → R bedrock
Protecting topsoil
  • Removing plant cover exposes topsoil to wind and runoff.
  • Roots, mulch, and contour practices slow erosion.

4.3 Soil texture, water, and nutrients

Soil particles and drainage
  • Sand has large particles and often drains quickly.
  • Clay has small particles, often retains more water and nutrients, and drains more slowly.
  • Loam mixes sand, silt, and clay.
  • Organic matter improves soil structure and nutrient retention.
Porosity, permeability, and pH
  • Porosity is the fraction of space available to hold water or air.
  • Permeability is how easily water can move through connected pores.
  • Soil pH affects nutrient availability.

4.4 Atmospheric layers

Composition and layers
  • Dry air: about 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen
  • Troposphere: most weather takes place here
  • Stratosphere: ozone absorbs much ultraviolet radiation
  • Mesosphere and thermosphere lie above the stratosphere
Temperature by altitude
  • Temperature generally falls with altitude in the troposphere and rises through much of the stratosphere as ozone absorbs UV radiation.

4.5 Global winds and rainfall belts

Circulation cells and rotation
  • Uneven solar heating establishes circulation cells.
  • Warm moist air rises near the equator, cools, and drops rain.
  • Drier air sinks near 30° latitude.
  • Earth’s rotation deflects moving air right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern Hemisphere.
Wind belts and rainfall
  • Hadley, Ferrel, and polar cells help explain trade winds, westerlies, and polar easterlies.
  • Follow a parcel up or down to predict cooling or warming.

4.6 Watershed boundaries

Drainage divides and runoff
  • A watershed is land that drains to a common outlet.
  • Topography defines divides.
  • Slope, soil, vegetation, and pavement control infiltration, runoff timing, and the pollutants carried into streams.
Tracing a pollution source
  • An upstream change can affect downstream organisms.
  • Comparing upstream and downstream samples can help investigate a potential pollution source.

4.7 Solar angle and seasons

Tilt, sunlight, and day length
  • Earth’s axial tilt changes sunlight angle and day length seasonally.
  • Direct rays concentrate energy over a smaller area.
  • A hemisphere tilted toward the Sun generally receives longer days and more direct sunlight.
Opposite seasons
  • The hemispheres have opposite seasons.
  • Earth–Sun distance does not account for that pattern.

4.8 Geography and climate

Water, elevation, and mountains
  • Large water bodies moderate nearby temperatures
  • Ocean currents redistribute heat
  • Windward side: air rises, cools, and drops rain
  • Leeward side: air descends, warms, and dries
The rain shadow
  • The dry leeward region is a rain shadow.
  • Higher elevations generally have cooler air.

4.9 El Niño and La Niña

Pacific winds and upwelling
  • El Niño: weaker Pacific trade winds and warm surface water shifts east
  • Upwelling of cool, nutrient-rich water falls off western South America
  • La Niña typically strengthens the trade winds and upwelling
Fishery and weather impacts
  • Lower upwelling can reduce marine productivity and fishery yields.
  • Weather effects vary by region and season.

Unit 5: Land and Water Use

10–15% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

5.1 Shared resources

Why shared resources get depleted
  • When users capture their own benefit while sharing the environmental cost, an unmanaged resource such as a fishery can be overused.
Rules that limit overuse
  • Catch limits, enforceable access rules, or community management can align private use with long-term resource renewal.

5.2 Effects of clearcutting

Effects of removing the canopy
  • Clearcutting removes most or all trees in a stand.
  • It can reduce habitat and carbon storage and increase erosion, runoff, sediment in streams, and stream temperatures.
Stream buffers and selective cutting
  • Retaining stream buffers shades waterways and intercepts sediment.
  • Selective cutting limits canopy removal but may still fragment habitat.

5.3 Green Revolution trade-offs

What boosted crop yields
  • Higher-yield crop varieties, irrigation, fertilizer, pesticides, and mechanization increased food production.
  • Productive systems can depend on more external inputs.
Yield and input trade-offs
  • Higher yield can reduce land needed per unit of food, while intensive irrigation or fertilizer use can deplete water or increase pollution.

5.4 Farming impacts

How farming changes soil and water
  • Tillage exposes soil to erosion
  • Monocultures can be more vulnerable to pests
  • Excess fertilizer adds nutrients to water
  • Pesticides can harm nontarget species
  • Intensive grazing can compact soil and remove cover
Tracing sediment runoff
  • Connect each practice to a mechanism and outcome.
  • For example, reduced soil cover increases erosion, which adds sediment to streams.

5.5 Irrigation methods

Four ways to irrigate
  • Flood: water spreads across the field
  • Furrow: water runs between crop rows
  • Sprinkler: water sprays overhead
  • Drip: water goes near roots, usually most efficiently, but with higher setup and maintenance costs
Waterlogging and salinization
  • Waterlogging limits root oxygen.
  • Where evaporation is high or drainage is poor, salts accumulate and reduce crop growth.

5.6 Pest control and resistance

Chemical, biological, and physical controls
  • Chemical: quick control, with potential harm to nontarget species and selection for resistance
  • Biological: use natural enemies
  • Mechanical and cultural: traps, barriers, and rotation
How resistance spreads
  • Pesticide resistance spreads because resistant individuals survive and reproduce.
  • Individual pests do not become resistant in response to a spray.

5.7 Raising livestock

Feedlots versus pasture
  • Feedlots concentrate animals, increasing production efficiency and creating concentrated manure and wastewater.
  • Pasture distributes manure but requires more land and can degrade soils if overgrazed.
Energy demand and methane
  • Energy losses between trophic levels make livestock production resource intensive.
  • Ruminants also emit methane from digestion.

5.8 Fishing pressures

Overharvest, bycatch, and habitat
  • Overfishing removes individuals faster than a population replaces them.
  • Bottom trawling can damage habitat, and bycatch kills nontarget animals.
  • Populations can decline before a fishery becomes unprofitable.
Fishery management
  • Quotas, gear rules, catch-size limits, and protected areas can reduce impacts.
  • Effective limits depend on monitoring and enforcement.

5.9 Mining and reclamation

Extraction and mine waste
  • Surface mining disturbs large areas.
  • Subsurface mining can cause subsidence and leaves waste.
  • Acid mine drainage occurs when exposed sulfide minerals react with air and water, creating acidic drainage that can mobilize metals.
Reclaiming disturbed land
  • Reclamation can restore contours, replace topsoil, and replant vegetation.
  • Managing tailings and drainage reduces ongoing pollution.

5.10 Urbanization and sprawl

Runoff, heat islands, and sprawl
  • Impervious surfaces increase stormwater runoff, flood peaks, and pollutant transport.
  • Dark surfaces and reduced vegetation produce urban heat islands.
  • Sprawl fragments habitat and often increases car use.
Cooling and compact development
  • Trees provide shade and cooling.
  • Compact development and public transport can reduce land and fuel demand.

5.11 Ecological footprints

Footprint versus biocapacity
  • An ecological footprint estimates the biologically productive area needed to supply consumption and absorb associated waste under current practices.
  • Biocapacity estimates what ecosystems can supply.
Per-capita versus total demand
  • Compare per-capita and total footprints separately.
  • Total footprint depends on both population and consumption per person.

5.12 Sustainability and resource renewal

Resource renewal and waste limits
  • Sustainable use meets current needs while maintaining future capacity.
  • Harvest of a renewable resource should stay within its regeneration rate, and waste inputs should stay within the system’s ability to process them.
Measuring long-term outcomes
  • Choose indicators that can be monitored, such as fish biomass, groundwater levels, or soil organic matter, and compare them over time.

5.13 Reducing urban runoff

Stormwater tools
  • Rain gardens and bioswales slow and filter runoff
  • Permeable paving increases infiltration
  • Green roofs intercept rain
  • Detention basins slow discharge and lower flood peaks
Peak flow versus runoff volume
  • These measures target different outcomes.
  • For example, a detention basin can reduce peak flow even if it does not substantially reduce total runoff.

5.14 Integrated pest management

Monitoring and action thresholds
  • Monitor → compare to action threshold → use preventive, biological, or physical controls → treat selectively if needed
A staged pest-control example
  • Rotate crops and protect predators, then use a selective treatment only when monitoring shows the economic or damage threshold has been reached.

5.15 Maintaining productive soil

Erosion and soil protection
  • Cover crops and crop rotation protect soil and cycle nutrients.
  • Contour plowing, terraces, windbreaks, and conservation tillage reduce erosion.
  • Intercropping and agroforestry diversify production.
Practices for sloping fields
  • On a slope, terraces shorten the effective slope and slow runoff.
  • Cover crops protect exposed soil and can reduce nutrient loss.

5.16 Aquaculture benefits and costs

Farmed seafood trade-offs
  • Aquaculture raises aquatic organisms for food and can ease pressure on wild fisheries.
  • Crowding may increase disease, escaped stocks may affect wild populations, and waste or feed inputs can degrade water quality.
Controlling waste and escapes
  • Recirculating systems reduce discharge and escapes but can use more energy.
  • Well-managed filter-feeding shellfish may remove suspended particles.

5.17 Sustainable forest management

Harvest and habitat practices
  • Selective logging and replanting help sustain timber harvests
  • Buffers and old-growth areas protect habitat and ecosystem services
  • A mix of species and tree ages supports forest recovery
Fire management and restoration
  • Prescribed burns can reduce fuel loads in fire-adapted forests.
  • Forest restoration also depends on suitable species and long-term monitoring.

Unit 6: Energy Resources and Consumption

10–15% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

6.1 Renewable and finite energy

Energy that replenishes versus finite fuels
  • Renewable sources replenish on human time scales if managed appropriately.
  • Fossil fuels and uranium are finite resources.
  • Renewable does not imply zero environmental impact.
Comparing sources
  • Compare sources by availability, reliability, emissions, land use, and effects of extraction and disposal.

6.2 Comparing energy use

Power versus energy
  • Power is the rate of energy use
  • Energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (hours)
  • Compare total consumption and consumption per person separately
  • Energy patterns depend on access, infrastructure, and economics
Electricity cost example
  • 1.5 kW × 4 h = 6 kWh
  • 6 kWh × $0.15/kWh = $0.90

6.3 Fuel uses and emissions

Coal, petroleum, and gas uses
  • Coal: mainly electricity and industry
  • Petroleum: mostly transportation
  • Natural gas: heating and electricity
  • Burning any fossil fuel releases CO₂. Coal commonly emits more SO₂ and particles than gas
Comparing emissions fairly
  • Compare emissions per unit of useful energy.
  • Include methane leakage when evaluating the climate impact of natural gas.

6.4 Distribution of energy resources

Geography and recoverable reserves
  • Geologic history shapes where coal, petroleum, gas, and uranium occur.
  • Sunlight, winds, rivers, and geothermal heat vary geographically.
  • A reserve is the part of a known resource that is recoverable under current conditions.
What makes a site practical
  • Practical energy potential also depends on access to land, transmission, water, and storage or backup.

6.5 Fossil-fuel extraction and combustion

Extraction and combustion impacts
  • Coal mining can remove habitat and create acid drainage.
  • Petroleum can spill.
  • Hydraulic fracturing uses water and generates wastewater.
  • Combustion makes CO₂ and may make NOₓ, SO₂, particulate matter, or mercury emissions.
Coal versus natural gas
  • Natural gas generally releases less CO₂ when combusted per unit of energy than coal, but methane leaks can reduce that advantage.

6.6 Nuclear fission

Fission, heat, and turbines
  • Fission → heat → steam → turbine → electricity
  • Control rods absorb neutrons to regulate the chain reaction
  • Operating emissions of CO₂ are low
Waste, cooling, and operating risks
  • Consider long-lived radioactive waste, cooling-water impacts, mining, accidents, and decommissioning when comparing costs and benefits.

6.7 Biomass fuels

Feedstocks and emissions
  • Wood, crop residues, landfill gas, and biofuels supply energy from organic material.
  • Burning biomass can produce CO₂ and particles.
  • Its net climate impact depends on regrowth, land-use changes, and fuel production.
Methane capture and land use
  • Capturing methane from decomposing waste can supply fuel.
  • Growing crops for fuel can compete with food production or habitat.

6.8 Solar technologies

Passive, photovoltaic, and thermal solar
  • Passive solar: building orientation and materials capture heat
  • Photovoltaic: cells turn light into electricity
  • Solar thermal: sunlight provides heat and sometimes electricity
Managing variable output
  • Output varies with daylight, cloud cover, and location.
  • Storage, transmission, or complementary power can help manage variability.

6.9 Hydroelectricity

Turbines, dams, and river habitat
  • Moving water turns turbines.
  • Dam reservoirs can provide electricity, storage, and flood control, but also flood habitat, alter flow, trap sediments, and block fish migration.
Ecological safeguards
  • A fish passage can reduce barriers for some species.
  • Maintaining downstream flow helps protect habitat.

6.10 Geothermal energy

Underground heat and heat pumps
  • Geothermal plants use heat from underground to generate electricity or supply direct heat.
  • Ground-source heat pumps use relatively stable shallow ground temperatures for heating and cooling.
Site constraints
  • Power generation is site dependent.
  • Potential impacts include water use, mineral-bearing fluids, and induced seismicity in some systems.

6.11 Hydrogen fuel cells

Hydrogen as an energy carrier
  • A fuel cell combines hydrogen and oxygen electrochemically to produce electricity, water, and heat.
  • Hydrogen is an energy carrier that must be produced first.
Life-cycle emissions
  • Life-cycle emissions depend on the hydrogen source and energy used to produce it, even though a hydrogen fuel cell emits water at the point of use.

6.12 Wind power

Turbines, siting, and variability
  • Turbines convert wind’s kinetic energy into electricity without fuel combustion.
  • Output varies with the wind.
  • Siting affects transmission costs, wildlife impacts, and noise.
Co-use and wildlife protection
  • Wind farms can share land with agriculture.
  • Careful siting and temporary shutdowns during high-risk periods can reduce bird and bat deaths.

6.13 Conservation and efficiency

Using less versus wasting less
  • Conservation reduces demand, such as using an appliance less often.
  • Efficiency provides the same service with less energy, such as using LEDs or improving insulation.
Efficiency and savings formulas
  • Efficiency (%) = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100
  • Energy savings = difference in power × hours used

Unit 7: Atmospheric Pollution

7–10% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

7.1 Primary and secondary air pollutants

Direct emissions and atmospheric reactions
  • Primary pollutants are emitted directly, such as CO, SO₂, and some particulates.
  • Secondary pollutants form in the atmosphere, such as ground-level ozone and many sulfate and nitrate particles.
Combustion products
  • Incomplete combustion → CO
  • High-temperature combustion → NOₓ
  • Sulfur in fuels → SO₂

7.2 How photochemical smog forms

Sunlight and ozone formation
  • Sunlight + NOₓ + VOCs → ground-level ozone and other secondary pollutants
  • Hot, sunny, stagnant conditions can make smog worse
Health impacts and controls
  • Ground-level ozone irritates airways and damages plant tissues.
  • Cutting precursor emissions from vehicles and industry can reduce smog, although the local chemistry affects the response.

7.3 Temperature inversions

Warm air over cool air
  • Normally, surface-heated air can rise and mix.
  • During an inversion, a warmer layer above cooler surface air suppresses vertical mixing and traps pollutants near ground level.
Why pollutants build up
  • An inversion can raise pollutant concentration without raising the emission rate.
  • Valleys and calm conditions can make trapping more pronounced.

7.4 Atmospheric CO₂ and fine particles

Sources of CO₂ and particles
  • Respiration, decomposition, and volcanoes release CO₂ naturally.
  • Fossil fuel use and land-use change add more.
  • Natural particles include windblown dust, sea salt, wildfire smoke, and volcanic ash.
  • Human sources include vehicles, power plants, and industrial activity.
  • Fine PM₂.₅ penetrates deeper into lungs than larger particles.
Warming versus health effects
  • Rising CO₂ drives long-term warming.
  • Fine particles can cause direct respiratory and cardiovascular harm, and some aerosols affect how much sunlight reaches the surface.

7.5 Indoor pollution and exposure

Sources inside buildings
  • Radon can enter buildings from soil and rock.
  • Incomplete combustion can release CO.
  • Smoke, VOCs, mold, and disturbed asbestos are other indoor hazards.
  • Risk depends on concentration, exposure duration, and ventilation.
Controls matched to each hazard
  • Match control to source: test and mitigate radon, vent combustion, use CO alarms, and address moisture that supports mold growth.

7.6 Controlling emissions

Devices for gases and particles
  • Catalytic converters: reduce CO, NOₓ, and unburned hydrocarbons from vehicles
  • Scrubbers: remove SO₂
  • Filters and electrostatic precipitators: capture particles
  • Cleaner fuels and transit reduce emissions at the source
Standards and source reduction
  • Select a technology that targets the pollutant.
  • In the U.S., the Clean Air Act provides a framework for air-quality standards and emissions controls.

7.7 Acid deposition

How acidic deposition forms
  • SO₂ and NOₓ → sulfuric and nitric acids → wet or dry deposition
  • Effects include lake acidification, soil nutrient loss, aluminum release, and damage to materials
Buffering and emissions cuts
  • Limestone-rich watersheds can buffer some acidity.
  • Reducing SO₂ and NOₓ addresses the cause.

7.8 Noise exposure

Noise sources and impacts
  • Road traffic, construction, aircraft, and industry produce noise.
  • Chronic exposure can disrupt sleep and communication, cause stress, and damage hearing at high levels.
  • Underwater noise can interfere with navigation and communication in marine animals.
Ways to reduce exposure
  • Barriers, quieter equipment, scheduling, and buffers reduce exposure.
  • The decibel scale is logarithmic.

Unit 8: Aquatic and Terrestrial Pollution

7–10% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

8.1 Point and nonpoint pollution

Pipes versus diffuse runoff
  • A point source is a specific discharge location such as a pipe.
  • Nonpoint pollution comes from diffuse areas, such as stormwater washing nutrients off many fields or roads.
Controls for each source
  • Treating a discharge at its outlet can control a point source.
  • Vegetated buffers and better land management help reduce nonpoint loads.

8.2 Aquatic ecosystem stress

Stressors in aquatic systems
  • Sediment increases turbidity and can smother habitat.
  • Microbes decomposing organic waste consume oxygen.
  • High biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) can lead to low dissolved oxygen (DO).
  • Oil and toxic metals can harm organisms directly.
Reading an oxygen sag curve
  • An oxygen sag curve shows a downstream drop in DO and recovery as oxygen is replenished.
  • Compare BOD, temperature, and upstream values before deciding what caused the drop.

8.3 Hormone disruption

Interference with hormone signals
  • Endocrine disruptors can mimic or block hormones or change their production, affecting development and reproduction.
  • Examples include some pesticides and industrial compounds.
Dose and timing
  • Effects can depend on dose and timing.
  • A sensitive developmental stage can be more vulnerable than adulthood.

8.4 Wetland and mangrove loss

Services at risk
  • These habitats filter water, store carbon, provide nursery habitat, and reduce flood or storm damage.
  • Draining, dredging, development, and pollution can degrade those services.
Restoring coastal and wetland habitat
  • Restoring tidal flow or protecting buffers supports recovery.
  • Mangroves can reduce coastal wave energy and trap sediment.

8.5 Nutrients, blooms, and hypoxia

Nutrient loading and oxygen loss
  • Extra nitrogen or phosphorus can trigger an algal bloom
  • After algae die, microbes break them down and use oxygen
  • Hypoxia or anoxia can cause fish kills
  • Some blooms also release toxins
The bloom-to-hypoxia chain
  • More nutrients → more algae → more decomposition → less dissolved oxygen
  • Reduce excess fertilizer and improve wastewater nutrient removal

8.6 Heat as a pollutant

Temperature and oxygen
  • Warm discharges lower water’s oxygen solubility and can raise organisms’ metabolic oxygen demand.
  • Sensitive species can decline as warmer-tolerant species become more common.
Cooling discharges and streams
  • Cooling towers or cooling ponds can reduce discharge temperature.
  • Shaded stream buffers also limit warming.

8.7 Persistent organic pollutants

Why persistent pollutants linger
  • POPs break down slowly, can travel far, and often accumulate in fatty tissue.
  • Some pesticides and industrial chemicals, such as DDT and PCBs, are examples.
Exposure after production ends
  • An exposure can continue after production stops because contaminated soils or sediments can remain a source.

8.8 Accumulation within organisms and food webs

Buildup within organisms versus trophic levels
  • Bioaccumulation is buildup in an individual when uptake outpaces elimination.
  • Biomagnification is increasing concentration across trophic levels for some persistent contaminants.
Methylmercury in fish
  • Predatory fish can have higher methylmercury concentrations than their prey.
  • Compare concentration in the same units before drawing conclusions.

8.9 Handling solid waste

Landfills versus incineration
  • Landfill: liners and leachate collection help contain waste. Landfills generate methane and need monitoring
  • Incineration: reduces waste volume and may recover energy. It also produces emissions and ash
Waste management and site cleanup laws
  • RCRA governs ongoing solid and hazardous waste management.
  • CERCLA (Superfund) addresses cleanup of contaminated sites.

8.10 Reducing the waste stream

Reduction, reuse, and recovery
  • Source reduction avoids making waste.
  • Reuse extends product life.
  • Recycling recovers material, and composting diverts organic matter into a useful soil amendment.
Limits of diversion
  • A waste strategy can reduce extraction and disposal, but collection, contamination, and processing influence its success.

8.11 Treating wastewater

Stages of wastewater treatment
  • Primary: physical removal of solids
  • Secondary: microbes break down organic matter
  • Advanced treatment: removes nutrients or other contaminants
  • Disinfection: controls pathogens
U.S. surface water and drinking water rules
  • Lower BOD reduces downstream oxygen stress.
  • The Clean Water Act regulates pollutant discharges to surface waters, while the Safe Drinking Water Act focuses on public drinking-water quality.

8.12 LD₅₀ and toxicity comparisons

Comparing acute toxicity
  • LD₅₀ is the dose that kills 50% of a test population under specific conditions, often reported in mg/kg.
  • A lower LD₅₀ indicates greater acute toxicity under comparable conditions.
Dose calculation and its limits
  • Dose (mg/kg) = mass received (mg) ÷ body mass (kg)
  • LD₅₀ does not show chronic effects or tell you how other species will respond

8.13 Interpreting dose–response graphs

Axes and response measures
  • The x-axis shows dose and the y-axis shows a measured response.
  • Depending on the graph, it can show mortality, a sublethal effect, or the percentage of a population responding.
Finding the 50% response
  • To estimate LD₅₀, find 50% mortality on the y-axis and read the corresponding dose.
  • Control groups help distinguish treatment effects from background responses.

8.14 Pollution and health risks

Routes, duration, and risk
  • Exposure occurs through inhalation, ingestion, or contact.
  • Acute exposure is short term.
  • Chronic exposure repeats or persists.
  • A hazard is the potential to cause harm, while risk depends on hazard and exposure.
Food-additive rule and exposure
  • The Delaney Clause prohibits approving food additives found to cause cancer in humans or animals under its scope.
  • Use exposure and dose when explaining why two groups may face different risks.

8.15 Infectious diseases and the environment

Transmission pathways
  • Pathogens include disease-causing viruses, bacteria, and parasites.
  • Transmission can be direct, waterborne, or vector-borne.
  • Unsafe water can spread cholera, and mosquitoes can transmit malaria.
Sanitation, vector control, and climate
  • Water treatment and sanitation address waterborne exposure.
  • Warming can shift the geographic range of some disease vectors.
  • Vector control and habitat management can reduce contact with them.

Unit 9: Global Change

15–20% of the AP exam’s multiple-choice section

9.1 Stratospheric ozone loss

UV shielding and ozone loss
  • Stratospheric ozone absorbs UV radiation.
  • UV breaks down long-lived compounds such as CFCs, releasing chlorine radicals that catalytically destroy ozone.
  • Polar conditions can accelerate depletion.
What increased UV-B does
  • Thinner ozone increases UV-B exposure, with consequences for skin, eyes, and organisms such as phytoplankton.

9.2 Protecting the ozone layer

Phasing out ozone-depleting substances
  • The Montreal Protocol phases out major ozone-depleting substances.
  • Recovery is gradual because some compounds persist in the atmosphere for decades.
Trade-offs of replacement gases
  • Replacing CFCs with compounds that do not destroy ozone helps, but some substitutes are strong greenhouse gases.
  • Evaluate both effects.

9.3 Greenhouse warming

Heat absorption and re-emission
  • Earth absorbs solar energy and emits infrared radiation.
  • Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some of that infrared radiation.
  • The natural greenhouse effect keeps the surface warmer, while rising concentrations strengthen it.
Greenhouse gases and ozone
  • Major greenhouse gases include water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide.
  • Ozone depletion and greenhouse warming have different mechanisms.

9.4 Why greenhouse gases increase

Sources by greenhouse gas
  • CO₂: fossil fuels, cement production, and land clearing
  • Methane: ruminants, landfills, rice cultivation, and fossil fuel operations
  • Nitrous oxide: fertilized soils
Warming potential and the Kyoto Protocol
  • Global warming potential compares a gas’s warming effect with CO₂ over a specified time.
  • The Kyoto Protocol set greenhouse-gas targets for participating industrialized countries.

9.5 Climate trends and responses

Evidence and effects of warming
  • Evidence includes rising temperatures, ice loss, and sea-level rise.
  • Warmer water expands and melting land ice adds water, raising sea level.
  • Climate change also shifts rainfall, habitat ranges, and the risk of some extreme events.
Mitigation versus adaptation
  • Mitigation limits the causes of warming, such as reducing emissions.
  • Adaptation reduces harm from effects, such as improving flood protection or managing heat risk.

9.6 Effects of warmer oceans

Bleaching, oxygen, and stratification
  • Heat stress can cause corals to expel their symbiotic algae (bleaching).
  • Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen and may strengthen stratification, reducing vertical mixing of oxygen and nutrients.
Coral recovery and range shifts
  • Bleaching can be reversible if stress subsides, but prolonged or repeated bleaching can increase mortality.
  • Marine species may shift toward cooler water.

9.7 Ocean acidification chemistry

Carbon dioxide, pH, and carbonate
  • Dissolved CO₂ → carbonic acid → more hydrogen ions → lower pH
  • More hydrogen ions mean fewer carbonate ions to build calcium carbonate structures
Effects on shells and skeletons
  • Lower pH can make shell and skeleton formation more difficult for corals, mollusks, and some plankton.
  • A one-unit pH decrease means ten times the hydrogen-ion concentration.

9.8 Invasive species

How introduced species spread
  • An invasive species is introduced outside its native range and causes harm.
  • It can spread when predators or pathogens are scarce, resources are available, and it reproduces or disperses successfully.
Prevention and early response
  • Prevent introductions through inspection and cleaning.
  • Early detection is often easier than controlling an established population.
  • Consider risks to nontarget species when selecting controls.

9.9 Endangerment and protection

Extinction risk and protections
  • Small populations, narrow ranges, specialized niches, and slow reproduction increase extinction risk.
  • In the U.S., the Endangered Species Act protects listed species and supports recovery.
  • CITES restricts international trade in listed wildlife and plants.
Recovery strategies
  • Recovery tools include protecting critical habitat, reconnecting populations, restoring habitat, and carefully managed captive breeding or reintroduction.

9.10 Human pressures on biodiversity

Drivers of biodiversity loss
  • Major drivers: habitat loss, invasive species, pollution, climate change, and overharvesting
  • Fragmentation also creates edge effects and can reduce gene flow
Conservation actions and indicators
  • Protected areas, corridors, restoration, and sustainable harvest target different pressures.
  • Evaluate success by tracking species abundance, richness, or habitat quality over time.

Unit 1: Chemistry of Life

1.1 Water and hydrogen bonding

Polarity
  • Oxygen pulls the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does.
  • Oxygen is partially negative and the hydrogens are partially positive.
Hydrogen bonding
  • The partially positive H of one water molecule attracts the partially negative O of another.
  • The O–H bonds inside one molecule are polar covalent bonds.
Cohesion / adhesion
  • Cohesion holds water molecules together and creates surface tension.
  • Adhesion makes water stick to other polar surfaces.
  • Both help water rise through narrow spaces, such as plant xylem.
Temperature
  • Water absorbs a lot of energy before its temperature rises much.
  • Evaporation removes higher-energy molecules, cooling the surface.
Water as a solvent
  • Water surrounds many ions and polar molecules so they can disperse.
  • Nonpolar substances such as oils tend to cluster away from water.

1.2 Essential elements

Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen
  • Carbon makes four covalent bonds and can build chains, branches, and rings. C, H, and O are common in all four major classes of biological molecules.
Nitrogen
  • Found in the amino group of amino acids and in the nitrogenous bases of DNA and RNA.
Phosphorus
  • Phosphate groups are part of nucleotide backbones, ATP, and phospholipid heads.
Sulfur
  • Found in some amino acids.
  • Disulfide bonds between cysteine side chains can stabilize protein shape.

1.3 Building and breaking macromolecules

Monomers / polymers
  • Monomers are smaller building blocks.
  • Sugars build polysaccharides, nucleotides build nucleic acids, and amino acids build polypeptides.
  • Most lipids are not true polymers.
Dehydration synthesis
  • Forms a covalent bond between building blocks and releases water.
Hydrolysis
  • Uses water to break a covalent bond.
  • For example, hydrolyzing starch can release smaller sugars.

1.4 Carbohydrate structure and roles

Building block
  • Monosaccharides such as glucose can link through glycosidic bonds.
  • Changing the bond orientation or branching changes the properties of the polymer.
Energy storage
  • Plants store glucose as starch.
  • Animals store glucose as glycogen.
  • Glycogen is highly branched, giving enzymes many ends from which to release glucose.
Structural support
  • Cellulose has β-linked glucose chains that form strong fibers in plant cell walls.
  • Humans lack the enzyme needed to break those bonds.

1.5 Lipid structure and roles

Triglycerides
  • One glycerol and three fatty acids.
  • Used for long-term energy storage.
Saturated / unsaturated
  • Saturated fatty acid tails have no C=C double bonds and pack closely.
  • Cis double bonds bend unsaturated tails and reduce tight packing.
Phospholipids
  • Hydrophilic phosphate-containing head and two hydrophobic tails.
  • In water, heads face the water and tails face inward to form a bilayer.
Steroids
  • Have four fused carbon rings.
  • Cholesterol helps buffer changes in membrane fluidity.
  • Some steroids act as hormones.

1.6 Nucleotide structure and nucleic acids

Nucleotide
  • Contains a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base.
Backbone / direction
  • Phosphodiester bonds connect the sugar of one nucleotide to the phosphate of the next.
  • Nucleic acid strands have distinct 5′ and 3′ ends.
DNA
  • Usually double-stranded and antiparallel.
  • Contains deoxyribose and A, T, G, C.
  • Complementary bases pair through hydrogen bonds: A–T and G–C.
RNA
  • Usually single-stranded.
  • Contains ribose and A, U, G, C. RNA can fold where bases pair within the same strand. A pairs with U and G with C.
Function
  • DNA stores genetic information.
  • Different types of RNA carry, interpret, or help use that information.

1.7 Amino acids and protein folding

Amino acids / peptide bonds
  • Amino acids have an amino group, a carboxyl group, and a variable R group.
  • Peptide bonds join the carboxyl group of one amino acid to the amino group of another.
Primary structure
  • The amino acid sequence, read from the amino end to the carboxyl end. A change in sequence can alter folding and function.
Secondary structure
  • Hydrogen bonds along the backbone create local folds such as α-helices and β-pleated sheets.
Tertiary structure
  • The overall shape of one polypeptide.
  • Stabilized by side-chain interactions such as hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions, hydrophobic clustering, and disulfide bonds.
Quaternary structure
  • The arrangement of multiple polypeptide chains.
  • Only proteins with multiple chains have this level.
Denaturation
  • Changes in temperature or pH can disrupt interactions that maintain shape.
  • The protein may lose function even if its peptide bonds stay intact.

Side chains and protein function

R-group properties
  • Nonpolar side chains tend to cluster away from water in soluble proteins.
  • Polar side chains can form hydrogen bonds, and charged side chains can form ionic attractions.
  • Changing a side chain can affect a local interaction, a binding site, or the overall fold.

Unit 2: Cells

2.1 Cell structures and their jobs

Structures shared by cells
  • All cells have a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, DNA, and ribosomes.
  • Ribosomes contain rRNA and protein and use mRNA to make polypeptides.
Prokaryotes / eukaryotes
  • Prokaryotes have DNA in a nucleoid region.
  • Eukaryotes have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
Rough ER / smooth ER
  • Rough ER has attached ribosomes that make proteins for secretion, membranes, or the endomembrane system.
  • Smooth ER makes lipids and helps detoxify substances.
Golgi and vesicles
  • Golgi sacs modify, sort, and package cellular products.
  • Transport vesicles carry those products to their destinations. A secreted protein can travel from rough ER → Golgi → vesicle → plasma membrane.
Lysosomes / vacuoles
  • Lysosomes contain enzymes that digest and recycle cellular material.
  • Vacuoles store substances. A plant cell’s large central vacuole helps maintain turgor pressure.
Mitochondria
  • The inner membrane forms folds called cristae, which increase the area for ATP-producing reactions.
  • The matrix contains enzymes for other stages of respiration.
Chloroplasts
  • Thylakoid membranes contain the machinery for light-dependent reactions.
  • The surrounding stroma is where the Calvin cycle occurs.

More structure–function connections

Ribosome location
  • Free ribosomes generally make proteins used in the cytosol.
  • Ribosomes on the rough ER make proteins entering the endomembrane system, including many secreted and membrane proteins.
Organelle membranes
  • Extra membrane surface lets cells fit more reaction machinery into a given volume.
  • Lysosomes can also help break down cellular components during programmed cell death.

2.2 Cell size and exchange

Surface area to volume
  • Surface area controls how much material can cross the cell boundary at once.
  • Volume reflects how much cytoplasm needs supplies and produces waste.
Calculate for a cube
  • For side length s: surface area = 6s², volume = s³, and SA:V = 6/s.
  • Doubling s makes area 4× larger and volume 8× larger, cutting SA:V in half.
Why size matters
  • A larger cell has less membrane area per unit of cytoplasm and longer diffusion distances.
  • Flattening, elongating, or folding a surface can improve exchange.

2.3 Plasma membrane structure

Fluid mosaic model
  • Phospholipids and many proteins move sideways within the membrane.
  • Proteins, sterols, and carbohydrate chains give the membrane varied functions.
Bilayer orientation
  • Polar heads face the watery cytoplasm and extracellular fluid.
  • Nonpolar tails form a hydrophobic interior.
Membrane proteins
  • Integral proteins sit in the bilayer and may span it.
  • Peripheral proteins associate with one surface.
  • Proteins can transport molecules, receive signals, or anchor structures.
Recognition and fluidity
  • Carbohydrate chains attached to proteins or lipids help with cell recognition.
  • Unsaturated tails reduce packing.
  • Cholesterol limits movement at higher temperatures and prevents tight packing at lower temperatures.

2.4 What crosses the membrane

Cross directly
  • Small nonpolar molecules such as O₂ and CO₂ pass through the hydrophobic core readily.
Cross slowly
  • Small uncharged polar molecules such as water can cross the bilayer slowly.
  • Aquaporins speed up water movement.
Require transport proteins
  • Ions and large polar molecules face a barrier in the hydrophobic interior.
  • Proteins let selected substances cross.
Cell walls
  • Walls provide support and resist swelling.
  • The plasma membrane is the main selectively permeable barrier.
  • Plants use cellulose, fungi use chitin, and most bacteria use peptidoglycan in their walls.

2.5 Passive, active, and vesicle transport

Passive transport
  • Net movement down a concentration gradient without direct energy input.
  • Simple diffusion and facilitated diffusion are both passive.
Active transport
  • Uses energy to move a substance against its concentration or electrochemical gradient.
  • Direct ATP use and coupling to another gradient are two ways to supply that energy.
Endocytosis
  • The membrane folds inward and pinches off a vesicle to bring material into the cell.
Exocytosis
  • A vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane and releases its contents outside the cell.
  • Both vesicle-based processes require energy.

2.6 Protein-assisted diffusion

Channel proteins
  • Provide a selective passage for substances such as Na⁺ or K⁺.
  • Some channels open or close in response to a signal.
Carrier proteins
  • Bind a particular substance and change shape to move it across.
  • Glucose can enter through a carrier when its gradient favors entry.
Aquaporins
  • Water channels that increase the rate of osmosis.
  • They do not change the direction of net movement.
Ion movement
  • Ions move down their electrochemical gradient, which combines concentration and electrical charge.
  • Their movement can change the voltage across the membrane.

2.7 Osmosis, tonicity, and water potential

Osmosis
  • Net water movement across a selectively permeable membrane from higher water potential to lower water potential.
  • Water still moves both ways at equilibrium, with no net movement.
Hypotonic outside
  • Fewer effective nonpenetrating solutes outside the cell.
  • Water enters.
  • An animal cell may burst, and a plant cell becomes turgid.
Isotonic outside
  • Equal effective nonpenetrating solute concentrations.
  • No net water movement.
  • An animal cell keeps its volume, and a plant cell is typically flaccid.
Hypertonic outside
  • More effective nonpenetrating solutes outside.
  • Water exits.
  • An animal cell shrinks, and a plant cell can plasmolyze as its membrane pulls away from the wall.
Water potential equation
  • Ψ = Ψₚ + Ψₛ.
  • Pressure potential (Ψₚ) increases with positive pressure.
  • Solute potential (Ψₛ) becomes more negative as solute concentration rises.
Solute potential equation
  • Ψₛ = −iCRT. i = ions or particles per dissolved formula unit, C = molar concentration, R = 0.0831 L·bar/(mol·K), and T = temperature in kelvin.
Example
  • If a cell has Ψ = −2 bars and its surroundings have Ψ = −4 bars, net water movement is out of the cell toward −4 bars.
Osmoregulation
  • Organisms regulate internal water and solute levels. A freshwater protist can use a contractile vacuole to expel excess water.

2.8 Pumps, gradients, and coupled transport

ATP-powered pump
  • The sodium-potassium pump uses one ATP per cycle to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in.
  • Both ions are moved against their gradients.
Electrochemical gradient
  • A difference in ion concentration and charge across a membrane stores potential energy.
  • Pumps build gradients that channels can let ions move down.
Coupled transport
  • The downhill movement of one substance can drive another uphill.
  • For example, Na⁺ entering down its gradient can power glucose uptake against its gradient.
  • The cotransporter itself does not split ATP.

2.9 Why cells use compartments

Different conditions
  • Membranes create distinct environments. A lysosome can maintain an acidic interior while the cytosol stays closer to neutral.
Local reactions
  • Compartments can concentrate enzymes and substrates, separate incompatible reactions, and add internal membrane area.
Example
  • The inner mitochondrial membrane separates the matrix from the intermembrane space, allowing a proton gradient to form for ATP production.

2.10 Compartment origins and endosymbiosis

Prokaryotic organization
  • Prokaryotes typically lack membrane-bound organelles.
  • They can still localize processes in specialized regions.
Endosymbiotic theory
  • An ancestral host incorporated bacteria that became mitochondria.
  • In photosynthetic eukaryotes, an ancestor incorporated a photosynthetic bacterium that became a chloroplast.
Evidence
  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own circular DNA, bacterial-like ribosomes, double membranes, and division resembling binary fission.

Unit 3: Cellular Energetics

3.1 Enzyme action

Activation energy
  • Enzymes speed reactions by lowering the activation energy, often by orienting substrates or stabilizing the transition state.
  • They are regenerated and do not change ΔG or the equilibrium position.
Active site
  • The substrate binds through chemical interactions related to shape and charge.
  • Induced fit changes enzyme shape upon binding.
  • At high substrate concentration, active sites become occupied and the rate approaches a maximum at fixed enzyme concentration.

3.2 Conditions that affect enzymes

Temperature and pH
  • Increasing temperature may raise reaction rate until loss of protein structure reduces activity.
  • Changes in pH can alter charges at the active site and disrupt protein structure.
  • Different enzymes have different optima.
Inhibition and rate data
  • Competitive inhibitors compete for the active site, so more substrate can reduce their effect.
  • Inhibitors binding elsewhere can change catalytic function and may reduce the maximum rate.
  • Compare initial slopes while controlling enzyme amount and other conditions.

3.3 Cellular energy and coupling

Energy transfer
  • Cells couple energy-releasing reactions to energy-requiring processes. ATP hydrolysis can drive transport, movement, and synthesis through phosphorylation or changes in protein conformation.
Energy pathways
  • A sequence of enzyme-catalyzed reactions makes energy transfer gradual and controllable.
  • Energy is conserved, but some usable energy disperses as heat.

Predicting and testing inheritance patterns

Probability and pedigrees
  • Multiply probabilities for independent events that must both occur.
  • Add mutually exclusive paths to the same outcome.
  • Use pedigrees and test crosses to infer candidate genotypes and modes of inheritance.
Linkage and recombination
  • Recombination frequency = recombinant offspring ÷ total offspring × 100%.
  • For nearby loci, 1% recombination approximates 1 map unit.
  • Frequencies near 50% cannot distinguish distant loci on one chromosome from loci on different chromosomes.
Chi-square test
  • χ² = Σ[(observed − expected)²/expected].
  • For a fixed predicted ratio, degrees of freedom are categories minus 1. A large χ² relative to the critical value supports rejecting the proposed ratio; failing to reject does not prove it.

Unit 6: Gene Expression and Regulation

6.1 Genetic information and strand direction

Base pairing
  • DNA strands run in opposite directions: A pairs with T and G with C. RNA contains U instead of T and can base-pair with DNA or RNA. A sequence must be read in the correct strand orientation.

6.2 How DNA is copied

Semiconservative replication
  • Each new double helix contains one parental strand and one new strand. DNA polymerase adds to a free 3′ end, so both strands are synthesized 5′ → 3′.
  • The leading strand is continuous and the lagging strand is made in fragments that are joined.

6.3 Transcription and RNA processing

Transcription
  • RNA polymerase starts near a promoter, reads the template 3′ → 5′, and builds complementary RNA 5′ → 3′.
  • The RNA matches the coding DNA strand except U replaces T, before processing.
Eukaryotic RNA
  • Processing of pre-mRNA can include a 5′ cap, a poly-A tail, and splicing.
  • Alternative splicing joins different exon combinations, producing different mature transcripts from one gene.

Post-transcriptional Regulations

  • Ensure mRNA stability
Adding poly-A-tail
  • Stabilize and protect mRNA

  • Removing introns, join exons together
  • Introns do not code for any amino acid sequences

  • Splicing some parts of exons too

6.4 Translation

Reading codons
  • A ribosome reads mRNA in the 5′ → 3′ direction in triplets. A tRNA anticodon pairs with a codon to deliver the appropriate amino acid. AUG usually establishes the reading frame; stop codons recruit release factors.
Making the protein
  • The ribosome links amino acids with peptide bonds.
  • The chain grows from the amino end toward the carboxyl end and can fold or be modified after release.
  • In prokaryotes, translation can begin while the mRNA is still being transcribed.

6.5 Regulation of expression

Prokaryotic control
  • In an operon, several genes can share a promoter and be transcribed together. A regulator’s binding can change in response to an environmental signal, altering how often transcription starts.
Eukaryotic control
  • Activators and repressors can act at regulatory DNA sites.
  • Chromatin accessibility, transcription factors, RNA processing, and RNA stability can all affect how much protein is produced.

6.6 Cell specialization

Same genome, different expression
  • Most cells in a multicellular organism share the same genome.
  • Signals and transcription factors turn on distinct sets of genes, leading to stable differences in protein production, cell structure, and function.

Population change and continuing evolution

Mutation and migration
  • Mutation introduces new alleles.
  • Gene flow moves alleles between populations and can make populations more similar.
  • Drift is random with respect to fitness, whereas selection changes frequencies because heritable phenotypes differ in reproductive success.
Continuing selection
  • Selection pressures can change through time. A changing environment can favor previously rare traits.
  • Individuals differ in survival or reproduction, while populations evolve across generations.

Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

Assumptions
  • 1.
  • Large population size (reduce genetic drift)
  • 2.
  • Random mating (eliminates changing alleles)
  • 3.
  • No gene flow (individuals cannot enter/leave population)
  • 4.
  • No selection
  • 5.
  • No mutations
  • To keep a stable allele frequency

Using the Hardy–Weinberg model

Alleles and genotypes
  • For two alleles, p + q = 1.
  • At Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, expected genotype frequencies are p², 2pq, and q².
  • If a recessive phenotype frequency is 0.09 under the model, q = 0.30, p = 0.70, and the expected heterozygote frequency is 0.42.
Model comparison
  • Use observed genotype counts to estimate allele frequencies, and compare predicted genotype frequencies with observations. A mismatch suggests a model assumption may be violated, but does not identify the cause by itself.

7.11 Variation in populations

Why variation matters
  • Heritable variation makes differential survival and reproduction possible.
  • Genetic variation can help populations persist when conditions change; the same trait can be beneficial in one setting and costly in another.

7.12 Models of the origin of life

Chemical evolution
  • Hypotheses propose that simple molecules produced organic building blocks, which could form larger molecules and become enclosed in protocell-like compartments.
  • Laboratory simulations support some steps and do not establish a single historical pathway.
RNA-first hypothesis
  • RNA can store information and some RNA molecules catalyze reactions.
  • These properties motivate the hypothesis that RNA-like systems preceded modern DNA–protein cells.

8.3–8.4 Population growth and density

Population change
  • Change in population size equals births plus immigration minus deaths and emigration.
  • In an exponential model, dN/dt = rN, giving a J-shaped curve at constant positive per-capita growth rate.
Logistic model
  • dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K).
  • Growth slows as N approaches carrying capacity K; in this ideal model absolute growth is highest at K/2.
  • Carrying capacity can change with resource availability.
Regulation
  • Competition and disease often intensify with density.
  • Events such as freezes or floods can affect populations regardless of density.
  • Distinguish the slope of a population-size graph from population size itself.

8.7 Ecosystem disruption and recovery

Disturbance
  • Climate shifts, habitat fragmentation, pollution, and invasive species can change community composition and nutrient or energy flows.
  • Loss of a keystone species can produce disproportionate effects.
Recovery and succession
  • Primary succession begins on a surface with little or no soil; secondary succession follows disturbance where soil remains.
  • Recovery depends on species interactions, disturbance intensity, and access to colonists.
Variation under changing conditions
  • Environmental change can favor variants already present; mutations do not arise because organisms need them.
  • When heterozygotes have greater relative fitness than either homozygote, selection can help maintain both alleles.

Unit 1: Kinematics

1.1 Scalars, vectors, and signs

Scalars and vectors
  • Distance and speed have magnitude only.
  • Displacement, velocity, and acceleration also have direction.
  • Choose a positive axis, then add signed components.

1.2 Motion with constant acceleration

Definitions
  • Displacement Δx = x − x₀.
  • Average velocity is Δx/Δt; average speed is total distance/Δt.
  • Average acceleration is Δv/Δt.
Equations
  • For constant a: v = v₀ + at; Δx = v₀t + ½at²; v² = v₀² + 2aΔx.
  • In free fall with upward positive, a = −g even at the highest point.

1.3 Reading motion graphs

Slopes and areas
  • Slope of x versus t gives velocity; slope of v versus t gives acceleration.
  • Signed area under v versus t gives displacement; signed area under a versus t gives change in velocity.
Speeding up
  • Speed increases when v and a have the same sign and decreases when they have opposite signs.

Velocity time graph
  • Object reaches highest point at v = 0 (x-int of graph)

Reference Frame

Definition
  • Axes used by observer to measure position, velocity, acceleration
Measurement
  • Velocity of the observe +/- velocity of object relative to ground
  • Acceleration is the same for any object

1.4 Relative velocity

Changing observers
  • v(object relative to observer) = v(object relative to ground) − v(observer relative to ground).
  • Observers moving at a constant velocity relative to each other measure the same acceleration of the same object.

1.5 Two-dimensional motion

Components
  • For a vector of magnitude v at angle θ above +x, vₓ = v cos θ and vᵧ = v sin θ.
  • Treat perpendicular components separately using the same time interval.
Projectile motion
  • With negligible drag, aₓ = 0 and aᵧ = −g.
  • For a launch and landing at equal heights, time of flight is 2v₀ᵧ/g and horizontal range is v₀ₓ times that time.

2.1 Center of mass

Position and motion
  • x_CM = Σmᵢxᵢ/Σmᵢ.
  • The center of mass accelerates according to ΣF_external = M a_CM.
  • Internal forces cannot change total momentum, and internal friction can convert mechanical energy to thermal energy even in an isolated system.

Ideal objects

Ideal string
  • - Negligible mass
  • - Does not stretch under tension
  • - Tension in ideal string same at all points within string
  • - Tension is different at all points with nonnegligible mass of
    string

  • - Negligible mass
  • - Rotates about axle through center of mass
  • - Negligible friction

2.2 Forces and free-body diagrams

Draw forces
  • Choose the object or system.
  • Draw only external forces acting on it, then resolve vectors along convenient axes.
  • Weight points toward Earth; a normal force is perpendicular to the surface; a string’s tension pulls along the string.

2.3 Newton’s third law

Force pairs
  • A’s force on B equals B’s force on A in magnitude and is opposite in direction.
  • The two forces act on different objects.
  • Weight and a surface’s normal force on the same object are not a third-law pair.

2.4–2.5 Inertia and net force

Zero net force
  • An object with zero net force stays at rest or moves at constant velocity.
  • Zero velocity at an instant, by itself, says nothing about net force.
Per-axis equations
  • ΣFₓ = maₓ and ΣFᵧ = maᵧ.
  • On an incline of angle θ, weight has components mg sin θ down the slope and mg cos θ into it.
  • Find the normal force from the perpendicular force equation.

2.6 Gravitational force and apparent weight

Gravity
  • F_g = Gm₁m₂/r² with center-to-center separation r.
  • The field magnitude a distance r from a spherical mass M is g = GM/r² outside that mass.
Scale readings
  • For a rider supported only by a floor and gravity, with upward positive, N − mg = ma.
  • In free fall N = 0 while gravity still acts.

Friction

Kinetic
Friction
  • Fraction contributing to moving object
  • Does not depend on size of contact surface area
    µ depends on material properties of contact surfaces
Static friction
  • Friction required to be overcome before moving
    µ of static friction > µ of kinetic friction

2.7 How to calculate friction

Static and kinetic
  • Static friction adjusts up to fₛ,max = μₛN and prevents relative slipping.
  • Once surfaces slide, fₖ = μₖN in the model, opposite the relative sliding direction.

2.8 Spring forces

Hooke’s law
  • For an ideal spring, Fₓ = −kx, where x is measured from its relaxed position.
  • The minus sign points the force toward the relaxed position. A force-versus-extension graph of the magnitude has slope k.

2.9 Circular motion

Radial acceleration
  • aᵣ = v²/r = ω²r points inward.
  • The radial component of the actual net force equals maᵣ. “Centripetal” describes that component, such as tension or gravity, rather than an extra force.
Period and direction
  • For uniform circular motion, v = 2πr/T.
  • Velocity is tangent, so the object accelerates even at constant speed.

Unit 3: Work, Energy, and Power

3.1 Translational kinetic energy

Definition
  • K = ½mv².
  • Kinetic energy is a scalar and depends on the speed measured in the chosen reference frame.
  • Doubling speed quadruples K.
Units
  • Measure mass in kilograms and speed in meters per second.
  • Energy is measured in joules (J).

3.2 Work and changes in kinetic energy

Constant force
  • W = Fd cos θ, where θ is the angle between the force and displacement.
  • Work is positive for a force component along the displacement, negative for one against it, and zero for a perpendicular force.
Variable force
  • Find the signed area under the graph of the force component along the direction of motion versus position.
Work–energy theorem
  • Net work on an object equals its change in translational kinetic energy: Wₙₑₜ = ΔK.

3.3 Potential energy

System
  • Potential energy belongs to a system of interacting objects.
  • For a conservative force, W = −ΔU.
  • The change in U is independent of the path.
Gravity
  • Near Earth’s surface, ΔU = mgΔh.
  • For two masses separated by distance r, U = −Gm₁m₂/r with zero at infinite separation.
Spring
  • Uₛ = ½kx², with x measured from the spring’s unstretched length.
  • Doubling the stretch or compression quadruples Uₛ.

3.4 Conservation and changes in energy

Mechanical energy
  • Mechanical energy = K + U.
  • When no energy is transferred or converted out of mechanical forms, a loss of potential energy is balanced by a gain in kinetic energy, and vice versa.
System boundary
  • Start by choosing the objects in the system.
  • Energy can cross its boundary through work or other transfers.
  • Track those transfers and all relevant forms of energy.
Friction
  • Mechanical energy can decrease as thermal energy increases.
  • For an isolated system that includes the rubbing surfaces, total energy is conserved.

3.5 Power

Average power
  • P = ΔE/Δt, or W/Δt when the energy transfer is work.
  • One watt equals one joule per second.
Instantaneous power
  • For a force at angle θ to the velocity, P = Fv cos θ.
  • At constant force along the motion, power increases with speed.

Unit 4: Linear Momentum

4.1 Momentum as a vector

Definition
  • p = mv.
  • Add momentum components with signs. SI units are kg·m/s, equivalent to N·s.

4.2 Impulse and change in momentum

Force and time
  • J = Δp = F_avgΔt, or the signed area under a force-versus-time graph.
  • For the same momentum change, increasing stopping time reduces average force magnitude.

4.3 Momentum conservation

Choose the system
  • Total momentum is conserved along a direction when net external impulse along that direction is zero or negligible.
  • This applies to collisions, recoil, and explosions even when kinetic energy changes.

Collision Types

Elastic collisions
  • 1. KE is conserved
  • 2.
  • Momentum is conserved
Inelastic
collisions
  • 1. KE is not conserved
  • 2. Momentum is conserved
Perfectly
inelastic
  • 2 objects stick together after collision

4.4 Solving collisions

Objects stick
  • For two objects that stick in one dimension, v_final = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁ + m₂) when external impulse is negligible.
  • For an elastic collision, also equate total kinetic energy before and after.

Unit 5: Torque and Rotational Dynamics

5.1 Angular kinematics

Displacement, speed, acceleration
  • Use radians and choose a positive rotation direction.
  • With constant α: ω = ω₀ + αt; Δθ = ω₀t + ½αt²; ω² = ω₀² + 2αΔθ.

5.2 Angular and linear quantities

Distance and acceleration
  • At distance r from a fixed axis, s = rθ, v_t = rω, a_t = rα, and aᵣ = rω².
  • All points on a rigid object share ω, but points farther from the axis move faster.

5.3 Torque about a pivot

Magnitude and sign
  • τ = rF sin θ = F times the perpendicular lever arm. A force whose line of action passes through the pivot produces zero torque.
  • Assign opposite signs to clockwise and counterclockwise torques.

Rolling objects

  • Mechanical energy = mgh + mv^2 / 2 + 𝑰⍵^2 / 2
    = linear PE + linear KE + rotational KE

Parallel Axis Theorem

  • 𝑰’ = 𝑰 + Md^2
    d is usually the radius of the object
  • Relates rotational inertia about any axis parallel to axis through its center of mass

Rotational Inertia

Hallow objects
  • Greater rotational inertia
  • Mass distributed further from axis of rotation
Solid objects
  • Smaller rotational inertia
  • Mass distributed closer to axis of rotation
Comparison
  • Rotational inertia of HOOP > rotational inertia of DISK > rotational
    inertia of SPHERE

5.4 Calculating rotational inertia

Mass distribution
  • For point masses, I = Σmᵢrᵢ² using perpendicular distances to the chosen axis.
  • In the parallel-axis relation, d is the separation between the two parallel axes; it need not equal a radius.

5.5 Rotational equilibrium

Balance conditions
  • Στ = 0 means constant angular velocity.
  • For a stationary extended object, set both ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0.
  • Choosing a pivot at an unknown force’s line of action can eliminate that force from the torque equation.

5.6 Torque and angular acceleration

Fixed axis
  • For a rigid body with constant I, Στ = Iα.
  • For a given net torque, a larger I produces a smaller angular acceleration.

Unit 6: Energy and Momentum of Rotating Systems

6.1–6.2 Rotational energy and work

Rotational kinetic energy
  • K_rot = ½Iω². A translating and rotating rigid body has K_total = ½Mv_CM² + ½I_CMω².
Rotational work
  • For constant torque, W = τΔθ.
  • Net work changes rotational kinetic energy for rotation about a fixed axis.
  • Area under the torque-versus-angle curve also gives work; instantaneous rotational power is τω.

6.3–6.4 Angular momentum and impulse

Angular momentum
  • For a body rotating about a fixed axis, L = Iω.
  • For a particle about a point, magnitude L = rp sin θ.
  • Angular impulse equals ΔL and is the area under a net torque-versus-time graph.
Conservation
  • If external net torque about the axis is zero, total L remains constant. A skater drawing arms inward increases ω as I falls; rotational kinetic energy can change because the skater does internal work.

Nonrigid System

Stretching
  • Greater mass distribution = greater moment of inertia
  • Larger radius = smaller angular velocity
Shrinking
  • Smaller mass distribution = smaller moment of inertia
  • Smaller radius = greater angular velocity

Rolling Without Slipping

  • Static friction causes rotation about center of mass
  • No relative motion between surface and point of contact on object
  • No kinetic friction is present
  • Constant angular velocity

Rolling While Slipping

  • Occurs when there is not enough static friction to allow object to roll
  • ZERO angular velocity

6.5 Rolling constraints

No slip
  • For a wheel on a fixed surface, v_CM = Rω and a_CM = Rα along the rolling direction.
  • Both v_CM and ω can change as a wheel rolls down a ramp.
Slip
  • A slipping wheel can still rotate; the contact point moves relative to the surface, so the no-slip equation need not hold.
  • Kinetic friction opposes relative motion at that point.

6.6 Circular orbits

Orbital speed and energy
  • Gravity supplies the radial force, so v = √(GM/r) for a circular orbit. K = GMm/(2r), U = −GMm/r, and E = −GMm/(2r).
  • Larger circular orbits have lower speed and less negative total energy.
Period and escape
  • T² = 4π²r³/(GM) for circular orbits.
  • To just escape with zero speed infinitely far away, initial total energy must be zero, giving v_escape = √(2GM/r).

7.1–7.2 Conditions, period, and frequency

SHM condition
  • A linear restoring force points toward equilibrium: a = −ω²x.

  • Ideal SHM period is independent of amplitude.

Unit 8: Fluids

8.1 Density of fluids

Definitions
  • Density ρ = m/V, usually in kg/m³.
  • Liquids and gases can flow and have no fixed shape.
  • The ideal-fluid model assumes incompressibility and negligible viscosity.

Gauge Pressure

  • Pressure exerted on incompressible fluid = constant total volume = constant density
  • Object underwater experiences pressure from the weight of fluid above the object
  • - Independent of shape
  • - Only dependent on depth of submergence

Pressure Difference

  • Pressure at top of block: ρgh + atmospheric pressure
  • Pressure at bottom of block: ρg(h+L) + atmospheric pressure

8.2 Pressure and hydraulic systems

Pressure
  • P = F_perpendicular/A.
  • In a fluid at rest with constant density, P_absolute = P_surface + ρgh and gauge pressure relative to the surface is ρgh.
  • At the same depth in the same connected static liquid, pressure is equal.
Pascal’s principle
  • An applied pressure change is transmitted through a confined fluid.
  • For ideal pistons at equal heights, F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂. A larger output force trades off against a shorter piston travel.

Sinking

buoyant force

  • Net force = downwards

  • Net force = upwards
  • Buoyant force = object’s weight

  • Object is submerged, but does not sink

8.3 Buoyant force

Displaced volume
  • F_b = ρ_fluid g V_displaced, directed up.
  • For a floating body at rest, F_b = mg and the submerged fraction is ρ_object/ρ_fluid for a uniform object.

Fluid Flow Rates

  • High pressure at end of pipe attached to tank
  • - Experiencing pressure from large weight of water above it
  • Low pressure at the other end of pipe
  • - Experiencing atmospheric pressure only

Fluid Flow Rates

  • Pressure difference causes water flow through the pipe

  • Fluid is incompressible

  • 3.

Container with Fluid Leaving an Opening

  • Velocity = 0 at surface
  • Opening height (y) = 0
  • Pressure at surface = pressure at opening = atmospheric pressure
    P1 = P2 = P(atm)

8.4 Continuity and Bernoulli

Continuity
  • Volume flow rate Q = Av.
  • Steady incompressible flow in a full pipe obeys A₁v₁ = A₂v₂, so fluid speeds up through a narrower section.
Energy along a streamline
  • For steady, ideal flow, P + ρgy + ½ρv² is constant along a streamline.
  • At equal heights in the same streamline, higher speed corresponds to lower static pressure.
Torricelli’s result
  • For a small opening a vertical distance h below the surface of a large open tank, v_exit ≈ √(2gh) when both points are at atmospheric pressure and the surface speed is negligible.

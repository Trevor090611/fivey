Unit 1: Basic Economic Concepts

Production Possibilities Curve (PPC)

Representation:
  • Maximum combinations given fixed resources and technology
Points:
  • Points on curve = efficient and feasible
  • Points inside curve = inefficient
  • Points outside curve = unattainable (unless exploit)
Slope of curve
  • Straight line = constant opportunity cost
  • Concave = increasing opportunity cost
Assumptions:
  • 1.
  • Economy only produces 2 goods
  • 2.
  • Resources fixed (labor, land, entrepreneurship, capital)
  • 3.
  • Technology stays constant (no advancement)
  • 4.
  • Resources used in their full potential
Consumer
goods
  • Created for direct consumption (food)
Capital goods
  • Created for indirect consumption, used to make other goods
    (machines)
Economic
growth
  • Capital goods production > Consumer goods production
    =more economic growth
Movement of
points in graph
  • Movement along curve = change in allocation of resources, does
    not change the curve
  • Movement from inside to edge = decrease in unemployment,
    increase efficiency, net economic growth
Problems and
limitation
  • 1.
  • Oversimplification, where there should be more than 2 goods
  • IRL
  • 2.
  • Resources are fixed, where there should be flux IRL
  • 3.
  • Technology is constant, where it shouldn’t IRL

Comparative & Absolute Advantage

Definition
  • Comparative = Producing at lower opportunity cost than another
    country
  • Absolute = can produce more products overall
Example
  • Country A produce 100 monster and 50 redbull
  • Country B produce 10 monster and 30 redbull
  • Country A has absolute advantage in production
  • Country B has comparative advantage in producing redbull
    (1 redbull = 1/3 monster in Country B,
    1 redbull = 2 monster in Country A)
Specialization
  • Country B should specialize in producing redbull
Price of
trading
  • In acceptable range of the 2 opportunity costs
  • E.g. 1 redbull should be sold for between ⅓ to 2 monsters
  • More than ⅓ , less than 2

Demand

Law of Demand
  • Price increase = quantity demanded decrease
Misconception
  • Demand = slope of the curve
  • Quantity demanded = point on the curve
  • PRICE INCREASE DOES NOT LEAD TO DEMAND DECREASE
Determinants /
shifters of demand
curve
  • 1.
  • Tastes / preferences
  • 2.
  • Income (normal goods vs inferior goods) [prefer normal
    goods when income increase    ]
  • 3.
  • Prices of related goods (Substitutes & complements)
  • 4.
  • Population
  • 5.
  • Expectations of future prices / income

Supply

Law of supply
  • Price increase = quantity supplied increase
Misconception
(same)
  • Supply = the slope of the graph
  • Quantity supplied = a point on the graph
  • PRICE INCREASE DOES NOT LEAD TO SUPPlY INCREASE
Determinants /
shifters of supply
  • 1.
  • Input / resource / production costs
  • 2.
  • Technology advancement
  • 3.
  • Number of sellers
  • 4.
  • Taxes, subsidies
  • 5.
  • Expectations of future prices
  • 6.
  • Prices of related outputs (multi-product firms)

Price ceiling

Definition
  • Legal maximum price set below equilibrium price
Effect
  • Low legal price = quantity demanded increase = quantity supplied
    falls
  • Less producers are willing to produce at lower sales price
  • Leads to SHORTAGE

Price floor

Definition
  • Legal minimum price above the equilibrium price
Effect
  • High legal prices = quantity supplied increase = quantity demand
    falls
  • Same opposite reason as price ceiling
  • Leads to SURPLUS

Unit 2: Economic Indicators and the Business Cycle

Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

Definition
  • Market value of all final goods and services produced
Intermediate
good
  • Good used to produce another good, not the final product
  • Avoided counting intermediate good since it will lead to double
    counting
Expenditure
approach
  • GDP = C + I + G + X
    C= consumer spending
    I = investment spending
    G = government spending
    X = net export
Income
approach
  • GDP as sum of wages, rent, interest and profits
Measurement
  • Measures domestic production

Real GDP

Definition
  • Total value of all final goods and services produced, adjusted for
    inflation
Characteristic
  • Constant prices from base year
Nominal GDP
  • Uses prices of the current year
Example
  • Year 1: Monster costs $5, 10 monsters were sold
  • Year 2 : Monster costs $6, 10 monsters were sold
  • Nominal GDP Year 1 = $50
  • Nominal GDP Year 2 = $60
  • Real GDP (Year 1 = base year) = $50
Meaning
  • Increase in nominal GDP makes it seem like there is an economic
    growth
  • However, Real GDP = Nominal GDP, indicating 0 growth

GDP per capita

Definition
  • Measure of country’s economic output per person
Characteristic
  • Shows standard of living, long term growth is defined as sustained
    increase in Real GDP per capita

Limitations of GDP

Cannot be
measured by
GDP
  • 1.
  • Income distribution
  • 2.
  • Underground / illegal production
  • 3.
  • Living standards

Difference between nominal GDP, real GDP & GDP per capita

Nominal GDP
  • Measures production of goods and services within nation’s
    borders
  • Increase output / increase price of goods and services produced
    = increase nominal GDP
  • Distorted by inflation
Real GDP
  • Measures production but price stays constant
  • Price change cannot affect real GDP, only change in output can
    cause real GDP to change
  • Removes inflation effect
GDP per capita
  • Production per person
  • Can show the standard of living of individuals

Unemployment

Frictional
unemployment
  • Short term, between jobs, entering labor forces
Structural
unemployment
  • Skill mismatch, technology changes, industry decline
Cyclical
unemployment
  • Economic downturns, lack of demand
Actual rate vs
natural rate
  • actual rate unemployment > natural rate unemployment
    = recessionary gap
    = potential GDP > actual GDP (shows underperformance)
    = a point inside PPC curve
  • Natural rate only has Frictional and Structural unemployment
  • Actual rate has all 3 unemployments
  • Actual Rate > Natural Rate = positive cyclical unemployment
    exists = economic downturn exists

Cost of inflation

Definition
  • Inefficiency and economic distortion created by rising prices
Shoe-leather
costs
  • People trying to counter effects of inflation (transactions avoid holding
    cash)
  • This waste valuable time and resources, which could have been used
    productively somewhere else
Menu Costs
  • Cost of changing prices to firms
  • Firms change prices to keep up with inflation (retagging items,
    updating digital menus)
  • This consumes resources (labour, materials)

Cost of inflation

WInner of
inflation
  • Borrowers
  • They pay back loans with cheaper dollars with less purchasing
    powers than they borrowed
Looser of
inflation
  • 1.
  • Lenders
  • Money received has less purchasing power than money lent out
  • 2.
  • Fixed income earners
  • Their income stays same while prices of products they buy goes up
Anticipated
inflation
  • People knows inflation is coming
  • Make adjustments (bank raise interest rates)
  • Cost of inflation will usually be shoe-leader cost and menu cost
Unanticipated
inflation
  • People dont know inflation is coming
  • Much more damage dealt due to less adjustments can be made in
    time
  • Leads to winner and losers of inflation

Business Cycles and Output Gaps

Expansion
  • Real GDP increasing = unemployment decreasing = inflation may
    rise
recession
  • Real GDP decreasing / below potential GDP = unemployment
    increasing = lower inflation = deflation may rise
Potential output
  • Level of real GDP when economy is at natural rate of
    unemployment
Output gap
  • Recessionary gap = actual real GDP < potential GDP
    Inflationary gap = actual real GDP > potential GDP

Consumer Price Index (CPI)

Formula
  • Total cost this year / total cost base year x 100%
Characteristic
  • Ignore price changes for nonconsumer items
  • Increasing in price of nonconsumer items can increase GDP
    deflator but does not affect CPI
Inflation rate
  • Change in CPI = inflation rate

GDP Deflator

Formula
  • Nominal GDP / Real GDP x 100%
Characteristic
  • Ignored import prices
  • Increase import prices can cause CPI to rise but does not affect
  • GDP deflator
Inflation rate
  • Change in GDP deflator = inflation rate

Unit 3: National Income and Price Determination

Aggregate Demand Model

Definition
  • Total demand for all finished goods produced
Formula
  • C + I + G + X
    C = consumer price
    I = investment spending
    G = government spending
    X = net export
Slope
  • Downward slope
Relationship
to GDP
  • AD = GDP in the long run
Real Wealth
Effect (C)
  • Decrease in overall price = increase purchase power = increase
    consumption = increase quantity of real GDP demanded
Interest Rate
Effect (I)
  • Decrease price level = less demand for money = decrease interest rate
    = increase investment spending = increase quantity of good demanded
Net Export
Effect (X)
  • Price level decrease = domestic goods are cheaper for foreigners =
    export increase = import decrease = increase in net export = increase
    demand for domestic production
Shift of AD
curve
  • 1.
  • Changes in the components of formula
  • 2.
  • Interest rates
  • 3. taxes

Short Run Aggregate Supply Model

Slope
  • Upward sloping due to sticky wages
Sticky wage
  • Tendency of nominal wages to adjust slowly to changes in economy
  • Nominal wage is the unadjusted salary of workers
Cause of
sticky wage
  • 1.
  • Workers have fixed wage contracts, prevent immediate
    adjustment
  • 2.
  • Minimum wage prevents wages from dropping below the line
Impact of
Sticky wage
  • Increasing price level = decrease purchasing power of workers
  • Labour becomes cheaper, firms hire more workers to increase output
    = positive slope of SRAS
Shift of
SRAS
  • 1.
  • Increase productivity = increase SRAS
  • 2.
  • Positive supply shock = lower input price/technological
    advancement = increase SRAS
  • 3.
  • Negative supply shock = increase input prices = decrease
  • SRAS
Stagflation
  • Decrease in aggregate supply
  • Caused by increase in resource price

Long Run Aggregate Supply Model

Slope
  • Vertical slope
Impact of
sticky wage
  • No impact in the long run
  • Wages and prices become flexible as contracts expire
  • Economy returns to potential output
Shifts of
LRAS
  • Changes in resource and technology

Output Gaps

Negative
output gap
  • Recessionary Gap
  • - Actual output < potential output
  • - Leftward shift of LRAS
  • - High unemployment
Positive
output gap
  • Inflationary Gap
  • - Actual output > potential output
  • - Low unemployment
Long run self
adjustment
  • Recessionary Gap:
    High unemployment = drop in wages = SRAS shifts rightward, back to
    full employment
  • Inflationary Gap:
    Low unemployment = wages increase = SRAS shifts leftward, back to
    full employment

Multipliers

Marginal
propensity to
consume
(MPC)
  • Change in consumption / change in income
Marginal
propensity to
save (MPS)
  • Change in saving / change in income
Formula
  • MPS + MPC = 1
Spending
multiplier
  • 1 / (1-MPC) = 1 / MPS
  • AD change = change in Government spending x spending multiplier
    AD change = change in tax x MPC x spending multiplier
Tax multiplier
  • -MPC / (1- MPC)
  • Negative since increase tax = decrease aggregate demand

Fiscal Policy

Discretionary
fiscal policy
  • Government changing spending / tax to close output gap
  • Shift ONLY the AD curve left / right
Expansionary
fiscal policy
  • Implemented for recessionary gap
  • Increasing government spending + decreasing tax = increase AD
  • Government NEVER shifts SRAS / LRAS to close recessionary gap
Contractionary
fiscal policy
  • Implemented for inflationary gap
  • Decreasing government spending + increasing tax = decreasing AD
  • Government NEVER shifts SRAS / LRAS to close inflationary gap
Automatic
stabilizer
  • Recession: tax falls = transfer payment increase = cushion fall in AD
  • Expansions: tax rise = transfer payment decrease = restrains rise in
    AD
Limits of
Fiscal Policy
  • 1. recognition , decision, implementation lag
  • 2.
  • Government raise interest rates, reducing investment
  • 3.
  • Changes equilibrium price

Unit 4: Financial Sector

Financial Assets

Definition
  • Claims on property / income of borrower
  • E.g. loans, funds, stocks
Liquidity
  • Speed of assets converting into cash without significant loss in value
  • High liquidity = cash & demand deposits
  • Low liquidity = stocks, long term bonds
Risk
  • Uncertainty of future value / income of the asset, chance of loosing
    initial investment
  • High risk = individual stocks
  • Low risk = government bonds / bank deposits
Rate of
return
  • Gain / loss of investment overtime

Relationships Between Financial Assets

Rate of returns
  • Higher risk = higher rate of return
  • Higher liquidity = lower rate of returns
Bond price &
interest rate
  • Higher interest rate = lower bond price
  • New bonds are at higher current rate = more attractive
  • Old bonds at lower current rate = must lower the price for
    competitiveness
Cash / Demand
deposits
  • - Highest liquidity
  • - Lowest risk
  • - Lowest rate of return (often 0%)
Bonds
  • - Moderate liquidity
  • - Moderate risk
  • - Moderate rate of return

Relationships Between Financial Assets

Stocks
  • - Moderate liquidity
  • - Highest risk
  • - Highest rate of return
Different assets
  • Firms hold different assets to balance immediate operational needs
    (high liquidity) with the goal of maximizing future profits (less
    opportunity cost and higher rate of return    )

Nominal vs Real Interest Rates

Nominal
  • Unadjusted for inflation, states rate
Real
  • Adjusted for inflation
Formula
  • Real interest rate = nominal interest rate - inflation rate

Money

Function
  • 1.
  • Medium of exchange
  • 2.
  • Store of value
Characteristics
of good money
  • - Durable
  • - Portable
  • - Limited supply
Commodity
money
  • Items having independent / intrinsic value
  • E.g. gold, silver, tobacco
  • Provides stability, but limited supply by limited resource, restrict
    economic growth
Flat money
  • No independent / intrinsic value
  • E.g.
  • Paper currency (dollar, yen)
  • Great flexibility for manage money supply for stabilization, but leads
    to inflation if printing too much

Money Supply Measure

M1
  • Currency in circulation + checkable deposits + traveller checks
M2
  • M1 + saving deposits

Banking & Expansion of Money Supply

Frictional
Reserve
banking
  • Bank keeps fraction of deposits as reserves, loan out the rest
T accounts
  • Assets vs Liabilities
  • Deposits = liabilities
  • Loans = assets
Money
creation
  • Bank make loans = create new deposits = increase money supply
Money
multiplier
  • 1 / required reserve ratio
  • Change in deposit = initial excess reserves x multiplier

Money Market

Y axis
  • Nominal interest rate
X axis
  • Quantity of money
Money
Supply (MS)
  • Vertical slope, set by central bank, independent of nominal interest
    rate
Money
Demand
(MD)
  • Downward slope
  • Higher interest rate = people hold less money = less quantity of
    money demanded

Shifting MS curve

  • By Central Bank actions, not real GDP nor price level
Open market
operations
  • Fed buy government bonds = increase cash in banking system =
    rightward shift of MS
Reserve
requirement
  • Decrease reserve requirement = banks lend more = MS shift right
Discount rate
  • Increase discount rate = more expensive for banks = MS shifts left

Shifting MD curve

Price level
  • Inflation = right shift
Real GDP
  • Increase real GDP = right shift
Technology in
payments
  • Increase transaction cost for converting non-money assets into cash
    = left shift

Loanable Funds Market

Y axis
  • Real interest rate
X axis
  • Quantity of loanable funds
Loanable
funds supply
(LS)
  • Upward sloping
Loanable
funds
demand
(LD)
  • Downward sloping

Shifting LS curve

Monetary policy
  • Expansionary monetary policy = right shift
Net capital
inflows
  • Net capital inflow into the country = right shift
  • Foreigners deposit more money into our country

Shifting LD curve

Government
borrowing
(deficit)
  • Government borrowing due to deficit = increase LD = shift right
Tax incentives
for investment
  • Investment tax credit = cheaper for firms to invest = shift right

Unit 5: Long-Run Consequences of Stabilization Policies

Fiscal & Monetary Policy in Short Run

Major effects
  • Shifting AD curve only
Expansionary Fiscal
Policy
  • Rightward shift AD
Contractionary Fiscal
Policy
  • Leftward shift AD
Expansionary
Monetary Policy
  • Used to fix recessionary gap
  • Fed buys bonds, decrease reserve requirement = decrease
    interest rate
  • Rightward shift AD
Contractionary
Monetary Policy
  • Used to fix inflationary gap
  • Fed sells bonds, increase reserve requirement = increase
    interest rate
  • Leftward shift AD
Policy lags
  • 1.
  • Recognition lag
  • 2.
  • Decision lag
  • 3.
  • Implementation lag
  • 4.
  • Impact lag
  • Cause mismatch timing between policy implementation and
    actual economic condition

Short Run Philips Curve (SRPC)

Definition
  • Inverse relationship between inflation rate and unemployment rate
    in the short run
Slope
  • Downard slope
Movement along
SRPC
  • - Increase AD (inflationary gap) = moving up (higher inflation,
    lower unemployment)
  • - Decrease AD (recessionary gap) = moving down (lower
    inflation, higher unemployment)
Shifting SRPC
  • Affected by SRAS ONLY
    Decrease SRAS = stagflation / supply shock = rightward SRPC

Long Run Philips Curve (LRPC)

Definition
  • No trade off between inflation rate and unemployment rate in long
    run
slope
  • Vertical slope at natural rate of unemployment (NRU)
Shifts of LRPC
  • Only shifts if there is change in NRU
  • - Labour force characteristics
  • - technology

Gaps

Inflationary
gap
  • Unemployment < NRU
  • SRPC on the left of LRPC
Recessionary
gap
  • Unemployment > NRU
  • SRPC on the right of LRPC

Quantity Theory of Money

Formula
  • MV = PY
    M = money supply
    V = velocity of money
    P = price level
    Y = real output
Long run
effect
  • V and Y are stable / constant
  • Increase money growth = increase inflation
Nominal vs
real variable
  • Nominal = measured in current market price
  • Real = adjusted for inflation, reflect actual purchasing power
  • Real variable = (Normal Variable / price index)(100%)
The Fisher
Effect
  • Nominal interest rate = Real Interest Rate + Expected Inflation
  • In the long run, nominal interest rate increase but real interest rate
    remain the same due to money growth = higher inflation
Hyperinflation
  • V rapidly increase during hyperinflation (act as multiplier for inflation)
  • - People rush to spend money on real assets
  • High M and high V lead to high P

Government Deficit and National Debt

Budget Deficit
  • Government spending - tax revenue
National Debt
  • Accumulation of past deficits - surplus
Financing
budget deficit
  • Government borrow money from domestic households / business /
    central bank →crowding out
Crowding out
  • Drives up real interest rate = discourage private investments = slow
    down long term economic growth

Unit 6: Open Economy—International Trade and Finance

Balance of Payments

Definition
  • Tracks economic transactions between countries
Current
account
  • - Net exports
  • - Income from abroad
  • - Net transfer
Financial /
capital account
  • - Purchasing final assets (Stocks, bonds)
  • - Purchasing real assets (factories, land)
Formula
  • Current account + financial account = 0
Credits
  • (+) sign on the account
  • Transaction that brings money INTO the country
Debit
  • (-) sign on the account
  • Transaction which money flows OUT of the country

Exchange Rates

Nominal
exchange rate
  • How much foreign currency you can buy with one unit of domestic
    currency
Appreciation
  • Value of currency increases, can buy more foreign currency
  • Foreign goods become cheaper = increase import
  • Domestic goods become more expensive = decrease export
Depreciation
  • Value of currency decrease, buys less of a foreign currency
  • Foreign goods become more expensive = decrease import
  • Domestic goods become cheaper = increase export
Exchange
rate shifters
  • 1.
  • Taste / preference (foreigners want more domestic product,
    increase demand for domestic currency = appreciation)
  • 2.
  • Price level (inflation leads to depreciation)
  • 3.
  • Income level (increase income = increase imports = increase
    domestic currency supply = depreciation

Foreign Exchange Market (FX)

Demand
curve
  • Downward sloping
Supply curve
  • Upward sloping
Shifters
  • Increase demand = appreciation = decrease supply
  • Decrease demand = depreciation = increase supply
  • Increasing import = increasing supply
  • Increasing export = increase demand for domestic currency =
    appreciation
  • Increase domestic real interest rate = attract capital inflow = increase
    demand / reduce supply
Monetary
policy link
  • Expansionary monetary policy = decrease interest rate = foreigners
    buy fewer bonds = decrease demand for currency = depreciation

Foreign Exchange Market (FX)

Net export
link
  • Depreciate = products cheaper for foreigners = increase net export =
    increase AD

Real Interest Rate & Capital Flows

Connect to loanable
funds market
  • Capital inflow = increase loanable funds supply = decrease
    domestic real interest rate
Interest rate effect
  • Increase real interest rate = capital inflow = appreciation =
    lower netexport
